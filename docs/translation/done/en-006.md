Natural History Museum
@@ FIELD seoTitle text
Natural History Museum: Halls, Dinosaurs, Meteorites
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Natural History Museum, founded in 1924 and renamed in 1992: Tarbosaurus, a giant claw, the Ajbogd meteorite, and halls on space, birds and Gobi animals.
@@ FIELD body richText
The Natural History Museum is in Sukhbaatar District of [Ulaanbaatar](/province/ulaanbaatar), northwest of the Government Palace. It was founded in 1924 as the "National Museum" and moved into this building in 1930. From 1956 to 1991 it was called the "State Central Museum," and since 1992 it has been the "Natural History Museum." It holds exhibits on Mongolia's geography, geology, minerals, plants, animals, paleontology and anthropology.

![image](asset:tic-img-ed424d7db1c9a90c)

## What are the key facts about the Natural History Museum?

- **Founded:** 1924, as the "National Museum."
- **Names:** "State Central Museum" in 1956–1991, and "Natural History Museum" since 1992.
- **Address:** Sukhbaatar District, northwest of the Government Palace.
- **Highlights:** Tarbosaurus, a giant dinosaur forelimb claw, the 582 kg "Ajbogd" meteorite, and items belonging to a cosmonaut.
- **Please note:** according to the source, the museum suspended its operations in June 2013.

## Where did the lions at the museum entrance come from?

The two stone lions at the entrance originally stood at the gate of the inner courtyard of the Winter Palace of the Bogd Khan. In 1954, at the initiative of the geographer and scientist O. Namnandorj, they were brought to the entrance of the State Central Museum.

## What is there to see in the geography and space halls?

### Geography hall

- "Simukov's Green Map," the first map of Mongolia.
- A map marking the travel routes of foreign scientists who studied Mongolia before the revolution.
- Distribution maps of [Mongolian fish](</about/nature/wildlife/mongol-orny-zagas>) and mammals.
- The "Ajbogd" meteorite, which is 92 percent iron and weighs 582 kg.
- The "Manlai" iron meteorite, which weighs 166.8 kg.

### Origin of life hall

Wall paintings show in chronological order how life began on Earth 4 billion years ago. Glass cases display soil and rock samples from each period.

### Space hall

This hall displays the clothing, belongings and notes of three famous Mongolians:

- **J. Gurragchaa.** One of the first cosmonauts from Mongolia and from Asia. He flew into space on March 22, 1981, and worked there for 8 days.
- **J. Tserendeleg.** A meteorologist who did research at the South Pole. Mongolia became the 34th country to do research at the South Pole, and this work lasted 367 days in 1972–1974.
- **G. Usukhbayar.** The first Mongolian to climb Everest. He began his expedition on April 28, 2005. On May 30 at 2:00 p.m. Ulaanbaatar time, he reached the summit and raised the national flag.

## What minerals and stones are in the geology hall?

Mongolia has more than 50 kinds of minerals. The hall displays the following samples:

- **Industrial raw materials:** fluorite, graphite, lead, tin, serpentine, soapstone, mica, quartz crystal, ore, phosphorus, sulfur, volcanic rock, marble and nephrite. These are used in construction materials, the chemical industry, porcelain and ceramics.
- **Food:** salt.
- **Gemstones:** garnet, emerald, rock crystal, amethyst, citrine, moss agate, agate, turquoise, lapis lazuli and jade, which Mongolians have long used for jewelry and decoration.

## What is there to see in the paleontology hall?

This hall has four main rooms and shows Mongolia's nature from tens of millions of years ago. Photography and filming are not allowed here. The main finds are:

- Petrified wood from 60–80 million years ago.
- Fossilized skin of a duck-billed dinosaur.
- Armored dinosaurs, toothless carnivorous dinosaurs, flying dinosaurs (considered ancestors of birds), thick-skulled dinosaurs, and ostrich-like carnivorous dinosaurs.
- The giant carnivorous Tarbosaurus, 14–15 meters long and weighing 2–3 tons.
- The thigh bone of a plant-eating sauropod.
- A giant forelimb claw. It is considered one of the rarest finds in world paleontology.
- A fossil of many young Protoceratops, a horned dinosaur, found together in 1995 by a joint Mongolian-Japanese expedition.
- A find of plant-eating and carnivorous dinosaurs that "died while fighting each other."

The north wall painting was made in 1981. It shows seven of the more than 60 dinosaurs found in Mongolia. You can also see fossil leaf prints, dinosaur eggs and nests, shells of freshwater and land turtles, skulls of a hairy rhinoceros, bones and skeletons, 70-million-year-old freshwater fish prints, a rhinoceros skeleton, bison horns, mammal jaws and teeth, ostrich eggs, and the remains of a mammoth. The mammoth died out about 10,000 years ago and was the giant mammal that lived alongside humans for the longest time.

## What is there to see in the plant and insect halls?

**The plant hall** shows native Mongolian plants as dried specimens, sorted by Gobi, steppe and forest-steppe zones. Plants are also grouped by use: medicinal, essential-oil, tanning, dye and food plants. **The insect hall** has dragonflies, grasshoppers, livestock parasites, fleas, beetles, butterflies, pests and spiders. Mongolia has 13,000 species of insects from 270 families.

## What is in the animal halls?

### Bird hall

469 species of birds have been recorded in Mongolia. The hall has mounted specimens of birds of prey such as eagles, vultures, falcons and hawks, waterbirds such as swans, cranes, ducks, geese and gulls, and birds of the steppe and forest. On the wall of the entrance hall hang the horns of argali, Siberian ibex, moose, red deer and gazelle listed in the [Mongolian Red Book](</stories/news/mongolyn-ulaan-nom>).

### Fish, amphibians and reptiles

Mongolia has 76 species of fish. The hall displays the [Darkhad whitefish](</about/nature/wildlife/darkhadyn-tsagaan-zagas>), the Khuvsgul omul, the Siberian sturgeon, taimen, lenok, grayling, pike, and snakes of springs and mineral waters, along with more than 20 species of lizards and their distribution maps.

### Camel exhibition

The exhibition includes handicrafts made of camel wool, camel-themed postcards, badges and posters, information about camel researchers, skeletons, a full mounted camel, literature, paintings and calligraphy. Humans domesticated the camel 5,000 years ago, and camel races were held 2,000 years ago during the time of the [Xiongnu (Hunnu)](</about/history/khunnu>).

### Mammal halls

- **Theory hall.** An exhibit showing the embryos, growth and development of mammals. Schoolchildren from general education schools do practical work here.
- **Forest and taiga animals.** Mongolia has about 140 species of mammals. You can see mounted moose, red deer, wild boar, brown bear, otter, mink, sable, stone marten, steppe polecat, wolverine and other animals.
- **Gobi animals.** The mounted animals include the wild Bactrian camel, Mongolian wild ass, Gobi bear, argali, Siberian ibex, snow leopard, goitered gazelle, Mongolian gazelle, Daurian hedgehog and Przewalski's horse (takhi). There is also an exhibit of tools from the herders' cooperative era, and a comparison of a skeleton of a person from that time with a modern person's skeleton.

## Is the Natural History Museum open now?

According to the source, the museum suspended its operations indefinitely in June 2013. It was reported that the building would be demolished and a modern museum building constructed in its place, and the exhibits were taken down and stored. Before you go, check the museum's current status, address and opening hours with an official source.

Deluun Boldog
@@ FIELD seoTitle text
Deluun Boldog: Chinggis Khaan's Birthplace, Dadal
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Deluun Boldog, Chinggis Khaan's birthplace in Dadal soum, Khentii, above the Onon and Balj confluence. Name meaning, scholars' views and legends.
@@ FIELD body richText
Deluun Boldog is a historical site in [Dadal soum](/province/khentii/dadal), [Khentii Province (aimag)](/province/khentii). It is considered the birthplace of [Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan)](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>). Historical chronicles record his birthplace as "Deluun Boldog on the Onon." Chinggis Khaan was born here in 1162, the Year of the Black Water Horse, to Mother Hoelun, wife of the warrior Yesugei.

## What is Deluun Boldog? Quick facts

- **Location:** Dadal soum, Khentii Province. Above the confluence of the [Onon River](</destination/landscapes/onon-gol>) and the Balj River, on the right bank of the Onon.
- **Shape:** A large promontory that juts toward the river and looks like a spleen (deluu).
- **Elevation:** 982.8 m above sea level.
- **Meaning of the name:** "deluun" means broad and wide, and "boldog" means mound or hillock.
- **Nearby:** Three Lakes (Gurvan Nuur), Bayankhaan Mountain, Balj Island, and the [Chinggis Khaan Wall](</destination/landscapes/chingisiin-khermen-zam>).

## Where is Deluun Boldog?

Deluun Boldog is a large promontory just upstream of the confluence of the Onon and Balj rivers. It extends northeast from the right bank of the Onon and juts toward the river. It really is shaped like a spleen (deluu), and this shape is clearer from a distance. On top stands a monument inscribed in Uighur Mongol script: "Deluun Boldog on the Onon, birthplace of Chinggis Khaan." The place called Deluun Boldog today is a wooded hill just north of the Three Lakes.

![image](asset:tic-img-d58e75f42761fe83)

## How did researchers identify Deluun Boldog?

Several scholars have proposed locations for this site.

### Prince O. Jamyan

Onkhud Sangajav's son, Prince Jamyan, was the first head of the Institute of Manuscripts. In 1928 he traveled along the Onon River and first suggested that Deluun Boldog lies on the northern shore of the Three Lakes in Dadal soum. He believed the names of the eastern and western lakes combined to form Deluun Boldog. He also noted another place. Southwest of the Khentii Mountain, on the west side of the Kherlen River, at the foot of Delgerkhan Mountain, there is a fat hill with dense forest called Deluun Undur. To its southeast, on the east side of the Kherlen River, a great road entering from the southeast is called "Boldagiin Am." Locals said Chinggis Khaan was born there.

### Academician Kh. Perlee

Kh. Perlee held that most of the many theories about Deluun Boldog are wrong. In his view, Deluun Boldog keeps its old name at the confluence of the Onon and Balj rivers. It is the hill with a stone monument near the Three Lakes spa resort, close to Dadal soum.

### Archaeologist Ts. Dorjsuren

Ts. Dorjsuren carefully compared many places. These include Burkhan Khaldun Mountain, Balj Island, the Tengeleg River, the route on which Borte was taken from the Olkhonud people by Yeke Chiledu, the Burgi shore at the source of the Kherlen River, the burial place and birthplace of Chinggis Khaan, the [Ikhes land](</destination/landscapes/ikhesiin-gazar>), and the Khuduu Island on the Kherlen. Like O. Jamyan and Kh. Perlee, he concluded that the spleen-shaped promontory jutting toward the river from the right bank of the Onon, above the confluence of the Onon and Balj, is the birthplace of Chinggis Khaan. He noted that this area was the core homeland of Chinggis Khaan and his ancestors. It is surrounded by places tied to many important events from before his time, during his life, and after.

## What is the legend behind the name Deluun Boldog?

According to legend, Yesugei's ger stood on an island in Dundakh Lake, one of the Three Lakes. The island is now underwater and no longer visible. Chinggis Khaan was born in the hour of the horse in a Year of the Horse, clutching a blood clot in his hand. Yesugei took this as a sign that a hero had been born. He slaughtered an unbranded roasting mare and fed his wife Hoelun a broth. Legend says the mare's spleen (deluu) was buried in the ground and called "deluun buldag," which became "Deluun Boldog." Because of this, the Eastern Lake was said to be called Deluun and the Middle Lake Buldag. A large black stone said to lie in the middle of the Middle Lake went under the water in the 1920s and is no longer visible. Locals say the lake level has dropped in recent years, so the navel stone of Boldog Hill may soon reappear.

## What are the Energy Ovoo and the Wishing Tree?

In 2012, an energy ovoo (sacred stone cairn) was built on the southern hilltop of Deluun Boldog. It was based on research by scientists from Mongolia, Russia, and Japan. Locals say the energy level is high here. Behind the ovoo stands the "Wishing Tree." Locals revere it and believe that if you whisper a wish to the tree and take energy from the energy ovoo before you leave, your journey will go well.

## What historical sites are near Deluun Boldog?

Dadal soum is a cradle of legend and history. Around Deluun Boldog are many places linked to The Secret History of the Mongols.

- **Chinggis Khaan Wall.** An earthen rampart that runs east to Manchuria through the Ulz River valley, south of the Onon River. Legend says a daughter-in-law had to stay out of her father-in-law's sight, so a high rampart was built and people migrated along both sides. Nomads rested at the places marked with long lines on the rampart, stayed at those with triangular lines, and camped overnight at those with square lines.
- **Incense Altar (Sangiin Tavuur).** It was named for the large incense offering made on the hill by the Onon River during the ceremony that enthroned Temujin as khan. Fragrant plants such as juniper, wild thyme, ganga, and Daurian rhododendron were burned.
- **Flag Ovoo (Bayan Ovoo).** Before going to war, Chinggis Khaan would place his Great Black Banner on the mountaintop. He called on the spirit of the sky, prayed, renewed his khiimori (a person's vital energy and good fortune), and struck his great drum to set his army in motion. Dadal soum was settled on the southern slope of this mountain with the ovoo.
- **Tunkheleg Stream (Tengeleg River).** This river lies 10 km west of Deluun Boldog. It is considered the Tunkheleg Stream often mentioned in The Secret History of the Mongols. The "Suvdan Sondor" expedition team determined it to be the Tengeleg River on Balj Island, where Bodonchar lived.
- **Bayankhaan Mountain.** This mountain, 30 km west of Deluun Boldog, has three peaks: Burkhant, Shurgadai, and Orgoit. Locals believe it is Burkhan Khaldun Mountain, where Chinggis Khaan fled and hid from the Merkit, survived, and vowed to "worship it for generations."
- **Maral Steppe (Maraan Steppe).** A wide valley that stretches north of Deluun Boldog, northeast of the Balj River. Legend says Maral, wife of the warrior Bartan, lived here.
- **Duwa Sokhor and Dobun Mergen Statue.** Located 12 km west, on the northern terrace at the end of the Tengeleg Stream, this is a very ancient standing stone statue.
- **Balj Island.** Located 11 km away, this is the end of the Tengeleg Stream and a bend of the Balj River. It is said that Bodonchar the Simpleton, the high ancestor of the Borjigin clan, lived here in a hut of grass.
- **Selbeg Hill (Selbegiin Dev).** About 20 km northwest, this was the place of a smith who repaired Chinggis Khaan's weapons, carts, and gear.
- **Uulzakhyn River.** A small river that flows north of the Onon River. It is said that Chinggis Khaan lined up his great army on its terrace and held councils about military campaigns. Later, Galdan Khan of the Oirat met the princes of Eastern Mongolia here. Over time it became the "Ugalzar River."

To read about the nature of the Onon and Balj river basin, see the [Onon-Balj](</destination/protected/onon-balj>) page.

Tsagaan Agui Cave
@@ FIELD seoTitle text
Tsagaan Agui Cave, Bayanlig: 750,000-Year-Old Human Traces
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Tsagaan Agui Cave is a 38 m cave in the Ikh Bogd Range, 40 km from Bayanlig soum. It holds traces of humans from 750,000–800,000 years ago.
@@ FIELD body richText
Tsagaan Agui Cave is a limestone cave on Tsagaan Mountain in the Ikh Bogd Range, in [Bayanlig soum](/province/bayankhongor#bayanlig) of [Bayankhongor Province (aimag)](/province/bayankhongor). It lies 40 km northeast of the soum center. Traces of human life more than 750,000 years old were found here, which makes the cave one of the oldest archaeological sites in Mongolia.

## What are the key facts about Tsagaan Agui Cave?

- **Location:** Bayankhongor Province, Bayanlig soum, Tsagaan Mountain in the Ikh Bogd Range. It is 40 km northeast of the soum center.
- **Length:** 38 m, with three main chambers.
- **Origin:** limestone rock.
- **Highlight:** large fluorite crystals.
- **Human traces:** dating from 750,000–800,000 years ago.
- **Research:** the "Mongolian Stone Age" project, field research in 1995–1998.

## What is the layout of Tsagaan Agui Cave?

The cave mouth faces southwest. Three connected chambers run one after another, for a total length of 38 meters.

- **Entrance:** 5.3 m wide, 7.9 m wide in its main part, and 4.6 m high.
- **Chamber 1:** 12 m long, 9 m wide, and 10 m high. The ceiling has a large opening, like the roof opening of a ger.
- **Chamber 2:** 7 × 8 × 9 meters. Large fluorite crystals have grown on the ceiling, and they sparkle when light hits them.
- **Chamber 3:** 5 × 4 × 4 meters. A passage 2 m wide and 10 m long branches off from the upper west side.

![image](asset:tic-img-66bcce4ab1131bb0)

## When did people live in Tsagaan Agui Cave?

Excavations have shown that people lived in the cave more than 750,000 years ago. Researchers also established that Mongolia and Central Asia had a warm, mild climate during the Quaternary period. Laboratories of the Siberian Branch of the Russian Academy of Sciences and the University of Arizona in Tucson, USA, dated the cave soil. Their results show that the lowest level of cultural layer III, layer 13, formed 750,000–800,000 years ago. Stone tools found in this layer confirm human activity at that time. Compared with classic finds from France, the tools proved to be made with Oldowan-era techniques. This showed that people have lived in today's Gobi Altai region for more than 700,000 years. Still, this date may not be the cave's oldest. During the deep excavation in the 1996 field season, the number of cultural layers kept growing, which suggests the dates could go back even further.

## What did the cave people make their tools from?

The cave's residents made tools from stones close at hand. About 5 meters northeast, above the cave mouth, there are traces of a workshop where tools were made from fine-grained red-brown and yellow stone. Preliminary findings suggest these stones may be a type of flint.

## What was found in the excavations?

The cave's stone tool finds are divided into three cultural layers by soil depth. All of them came from excavations I–III and from test pit II.

### Cultural layer I (levels 1–2)

This layer lies in the upper part of the soil. A total of 74 artifacts were found:

- 2 pieces of production waste, 1 flat stone with waste, 1 small blade, 54 flakes, and 16 fragments struck from cores.
- Of these, 11 (14.9 percent) are tools: 2 scrapers, 1 burin tool, 2 multipurpose tools, 3 edged items, 2 handled tools, and 1 point or awl.

### Cultural layer II (levels 3–8)

This layer lies in the middle of the excavation. A total of 241 artifacts were found:

- 1 core split on the orthogonal principle, 1 Levallois core preparation piece, 6 fragments struck from cores, 1 split pebble, 2 primary waste pieces, 3 pieces of production waste, 1 Levallois point, 1 Levallois blade, 1 middle section of a blade with two facets, 2 elongated truncated waste pieces, 129 flakes, and 92 fragments of various types.
- Of these, 32 (13.3 percent) are tools: 1 Levallois point, 1 sentinel tool, 2 small scrapers, 5 burin tools, 1 scraper, 4 tools with serrated edges, 1 point or awl, 3 multipurpose tools, 1 core scraper, 8 classic-shaped tools, 4 edged items, and 1 tool with a splitting edge.

### Cultural layer III (levels 9–13)

This is the deepest layer. A total of 295 artifacts were found:

- 1 core preparation piece with a single striking platform for blade removal from one side, 11 fragments struck from cores, 146 flakes, and 137 fragments of various types.
- Of these, 22 (7.5 percent) are tools: 2 scrapers, 1 item with a serrated edge, 1 point or awl, 4 multipurpose tools, 3 small scrapers, 1 burin item, 1 classic tool, and 7 edged items.

The finds from the lowest level, layer 13, look considerably older than other stone tool sites used for comparison.

## What do the archaeological terms for the finds mean?

Here is a short guide to the archaeological terms used in the excavation report:

- **Core.** The source stone from which flakes were struck to make tools. Fragments struck from cores are pieces created during this work.
- **Flake.** A thin piece struck off a core. Most of the finds at Tsagaan Agui are flakes.
- **Blade.** A long, narrow piece with parallel edges.
- **Waste piece.** Stone or fragments discarded while a tool was being made.
- **Scraper.** An edged tool used to scrape hides and wood.
- **Burin.** A sharp-pointed tool used to carve wood and bone and to cut grooves.
- **Levallois technique.** A method in which a core is prepared in advance, and a piece of a set shape is removed with a single blow. Points and blades made this way were found in layer II.

The share of tools falls with depth: 14.9 percent in layer I, 13.3 percent in layer II, and 7.5 percent in layer III. Most finds are flakes and fragments left over from toolmaking, which shows that stone tools were made on the spot in the cave.

## Why is Tsagaan Agui Cave important?

The finds from Tsagaan Agui Cave are rare evidence that pushes back the date when people first settled in Central Asia. The three cultural layers show that people used this cave as a home again and again over hundreds of thousands of years. They also prove that today's Gobi region had a warm, mild climate during the Quaternary period, which matters for the study of ancient environments.

## Who researched Tsagaan Agui Cave?

The site was discovered during the 1995–1998 field research of the "Mongolian Stone Age" project, which aimed to clarify when people first settled in Central Asia. Scientists from Russia and the USA carried out the dating research. For more on Mongolia's Stone Age, read about [Stone Age sites](</about/history/chuluun-zevsgiin-ueiin-dursgal>) separately. For the nearby mountains, see the [Ikh Bogd Mountain](</destination/protected/ikh-bogd-uul>) page.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is Tsagaan Agui Cave?

It is on Tsagaan Mountain in the Ikh Bogd Range, 40 km northeast of the center of Bayanlig soum in Bayankhongor Province.

### When did people live in Tsagaan Agui Cave?

Laboratory tests show that the lowest cultural layer of the cave formed 750,000–800,000 years ago, and people were active here at that time.

### How long is Tsagaan Agui Cave?

It is 38 meters long, with three chambers. The largest is 12 m long, 9 m wide, and 10 m high.

Buir Lake: Largest Lake in Eastern Mongolia
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Buir Lake covers 615 km² with 36 fish species, fed by the Khalkh River and drained by the Orshuun. Learn its legend and Chinggis Khaan's farming decree.
@@ FIELD body richText
Buir Lake is the largest lake in eastern Mongolia. This freshwater lake lies in [Dornod Province (aimag)](/province/dornod) and covers 615 km². It is rich in fish, so it is one of the main sources of income for local people. In the 12th–14th centuries, the Buir Lake area was a major farming center.

## What are the key facts about Buir Lake?

- **Area:** 615 km².
- **Size:** 40 km long from northeast to southwest, 21 km wide, with a shoreline of 113 km.
- **Catchment area:** 20,200 km².
- **Depth:** 6–10 m on average.
- **Rivers:** The [Khalkh River](</destination/landscapes/khalkh-gol>) flows in, and the Orshuun River flows out.
- **Fish:** 36 species from 6 families.

## What makes Buir Lake special?

Along the southern shore, sand bars 3–5 meters high have formed from wave action. Small lakes have formed between them, and some are connected to the main lake. There are many lakes and ponds in eastern Mongolia, but Buir Lake is the largest of them. Because the Khalkh River flows in and the Orshuun River flows out, willows and reeds grow thickly along the shore. The water of Buir Lake is very fresh. Bicarbonate, chloride, sodium and calcium dominate the water.

![image](asset:tic-img-686625b655904f29)

## What fish live in Buir Lake?

The lake has 36 fish species from 6 families and 49 species of phytoplankton on record. The main fish are:

- [Whitefish](</about/nature/wildlife/buluu-tsagaan>)
- Pike
- Carp
- Amur sazan
- Red-finned fish
- Whitefish (olgor tsagaan)
- Tsulbuurt
- Silver-tongued fish

According to the sources, a crayfish called "Leander," listed in the Red Book, lives in Buir Lake. It is noted as found only in this lake. Thanks to this abundance, fishing has long been one of the main sources of income for local people.

## What is the legend of Buir Lake?

The Khulun-Buir area is a source of pride for its shimmering Khulun Lake, its towering Khyangan Pass and its wide steppe full of flowers. A legend explains the name of this land. Long ago, a Mongol clan lived on the clear steppe, herding livestock. There lived a girl named Khulun, whose beauty put flowers to shame and whose slender back made birch trees envious. There also lived a man named Buir, with a body as mighty as a mountain and the strength of a lion and a tiger. Among their native pastures they herded the five types of livestock, milked their cows and mares for yogurt and airag (fermented mare's milk), and lived free and happy. But as the saying goes, "misfortune comes from under the ground, and the pig from inside the tree," and sorrow arrived suddenly. One day a black, fifteen-headed monster arrived in their land. It stirred up gusty winds, brought heavy downpours, and raised red dust on the horizon. The monster seized the girl Khulun and drank rivers dry. Wherever it went, grass stopped growing, and people and livestock were dying of thirst, close to death. Seeing this, Buir mounted his swift horse, took up his long sword, and set out to defeat the dangerous monster, free his land and rescue his dear Khulun. Like an arrow shot from a bow and like a soaring eagle, he rode from sunrise to sunset, from spring to autumn, and from winter to summer. One day he took off the saddle to rest his horse's back and let it graze. In a sudden dream he saw Khulun. The monster had turned Khulun, who resisted, into a lily and tormented her in the sun. Buir poured the one gulp of water he had kept to stay alive over the flower, and the flower turned back into Khulun. But the monster seized the girl again. Buir collected his wits, took the precious pearl set in the monster's head, and killed it, saving his land from danger. But he searched everywhere for Khulun, who had been taken by the monster, and could not find her. As he walked in anguish along the shore of a clear lake, people told him that Khulun, unable to endure the monster's oppression, had turned into a lake. To stay with his beloved forever, Buir leaped into the lake. Since then, another lake appeared next to Khulun Lake, and it is said to have been named Buir Lake.

## Why did Chinggis Khaan make the Buir Lake area farmland?

[Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan)](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>) traveled through the Buir Lake area many times, so he knew this land well. Historical sources record that he issued a decree to turn this well-watered, fertile land into farmland.

- **1202, the Year of the Dog.** While Temujin was fighting near the Ergune River, he ordered Gulur Ondurkhin, Anchin and Khasar of the Khongirad people to settle in places such as Khemur Undur (Khamar Pass), Dala Naur (Dalai Lake), Timiger, Gemegen Khee and the Khalkh River, and to make them farmland. There were already people farming around Buir Lake before this, so he seems to have chosen this area for that reason.
- **1223.** The Chinese scholar Chang Chun, on his way from China to Mongolia, crossed a strip of sand on March 1 and reached Buir Lake. He noted that many people there were farming. This was a continuation of the farming that began in 1202.
- **1295.** When crops failed among the Tatar people and famine struck, the royal treasury of the Yuan Dynasty provided one million strings of paper money and silk. This shows that farming at Buir Lake continued until the early 14th century and played an important role in the lives of the people there.

Such a large amount of aid shows that farming was the main source of income for the local population at that time. It is believed that there were large trading settlements around Buir Lake and in the neighboring lands. There, Tatar farmers could take that paper money and receive the goods they needed.

## What crops did the ancient Mongols grow?

These facts suggest that the earliest cultivated plant in Mongolia was millet. Even today, wild umbrella millet grows commonly in this area. In the favorable weather of that time, people sowed local seed of umbrella and head millet. Later, Chinese historical sources described it as "coarse." Researchers take this as evidence that simple farming arose independently in Mongolia, without influence from the south.

It covers 615 km², is 40 km long and 21 km wide, and has a shoreline of 113 km. It is the largest lake in eastern Mongolia.

### How many fish species live in Buir Lake?

36 species from 6 families are on record. Whitefish, pike, carp and silver-tongued fish are common.

In 1202, Temujin ordered that this land become farmland. Historical sources show that farming continued until the early 14th century.

The Kul Tegin Monument is in [Arkhangai Province](/province/arkhangai) (aimag), in [Khashaat soum](</province/arkhangai#khashaat>). It stands 47 km north of Kharkhorin, on the east bank of the Khogshin Orkhon River, southwest of Tsaidam Lake. It is known as the "Khushuu Tsaidam Monument" and the "Orkhon Valley Monument," and researchers have studied it for more than 100 years.

## What are the key facts about the Kul Tegin Monument?

- **Location:** Arkhangai Province, Khashaat soum, 47 km north of Kharkhorin, southwest of Tsaidam Lake.
- **Erected:** 732, by Bilge Khagan.
- **Size:** 3.33 m high, 1.32 m wide, 0.46 m thick. With its turtle-shaped stone base, it is 3.83 m tall.
- **Inscription:** a runic inscription of 68 lines and about 10,000 characters, carved on three sides.
- **Excavation:** In 1958, a joint Mongolian-Czechoslovak team excavated almost the entire complex.

## Who was Kul Tegin?

Kul Tegin was a general of the Turkic state. Sources say he was born in 684. He began serving the state at age 16 and was a wise, gifted general. He died in 731.

### How did Bilge Khagan come to the throne?

In 716, Qapaghan Khagan of the Second Turkic Khaganate was killed by rebels at his palace on the bank of the Tuul River. The throne should have gone to Bilge, the eldest son of Elteres Khagan, who had received the title "Shad of the Tardush" in 698. But Qapaghan's senior officials placed his son Bögü on the throne. Bilge's younger brother Kul Tegin would not accept this injustice. He attacked the palace, killed Bögü Khagan, and placed his older brother Bilge on the throne. After that, Kul Tegin became the lord of the eastern wing and commander of all troops, and he led the state's military affairs.

### How did he save the state in a hard time?

Bilge Khagan took the throne when the state his father had built was close to collapse. Subject tribes had rebelled, and even the core Turkic tribes were in turmoil. Bilge offered to give the throne to his younger brother, but Kul Tegin did not want to break the traditional rule of succession. Bilge made the decisions, but real power was in Kul Tegin's hands. On Kul Tegin's orders, Qapaghan Khagan's relatives and close associates were removed from office and killed. Only the adviser Tonyukuk was spared. Tonyukuk was Bilge Khagan's father-in-law, and the people respected and feared him. After some time, he was made the state's adviser. In this way, the two brothers put down the uprisings, brought peace to the state, and carried out the great affairs of state together.

## What did the memorial complex look like?

When Kul Tegin died, Bilge Khagan mourned deeply. In 732 he had a radiant monument, a sacred memorial complex, and a shrine built.

- **Wall:** A brick wall 1 m thick surrounded an area 67 m long and 29 m wide, with a ditch dug outside it.
- **Floor:** The whole area inside the wall was paved with bricks measuring 32×32×6 cm.
- **Position of the monument:** It was raised at the center of the complex on a turtle-shaped stone, with its front facing the rising sun.
- **Finds:** clay vessels, iron household and decorative items, pieces of tile roofing, a pillar base, and pottery. The statues show the faces, clothing, and ornaments of people of that time in realistic detail.

## How is the monument decorated?

The top of the monument is decorated with two coiled dragons. In the center, above the inscription, is a carved ibex, the tamga (clan emblem) of the Ashina clan. On its three sides are 68 lines of about 10,000 runic characters. Researchers call lines 1–13 the "short inscription" and lines 14–68 the "long inscription."

![image](asset:tic-img-8b13b9fdde8b5eb7)

The turtle-shaped base stone also bears a Turkic inscription. It was first studied in 1979, and B. Bazylkhan translated the text. Some parts are so faded that they could not be read in full.

## What does the inscription say?

Kul Tegin's grandnephew, Yollug Tegin, carved the inscription over 20 days. It tells of the following:

- How the Turkic state was founded.
- How Kul Tegin took part in state affairs from age 16, which tribes and states he conquered, how he fought, and what colors of horses he rode.
- How he served his state day and night, made the poor rich and the orphaned numerous, ruled a vast land from sunrise to sunset, and built a strong state.
- How Bilge Khagan grieved at Kul Tegin's burial, and the foreign guests who came to the funeral.

At the end, the inscription reads "Bo bitig bitigme atysy Yollug tigin," meaning "the one who wrote this inscription is (Kul Tegin's) grandnephew Yollug Tegin."

## What lessons does the inscription teach?

The inscription is full of words meant as lessons for later generations. It recalls that when half of the Turks wanted to move south, some people persuaded them that the Tabgach (Chinese) state would give them fine goods if they came near. It warns that many Turkic tribes perished because of such foolish people: "...Unwise people went close to the Tabgach state, and many Turkic clans and tribes perished..." The short inscription teaches that if the Turks stay in the forested highlands of Otuken, they will rule their state forever: "...If you stay in the land of Otuken and send out your caravans from there (oh Turk, you) have no trouble at all. If you stay in the Otuken highlands, you will rule your state forever..." It stresses this idea further and recalls the comfortable life when the state was united: "..Oh Turkic people, well fed ... truly you did not know hunger (hardship) ... that is how it was, so I united the Turkic tribes and brought them peace. ... In those days our slaves (descendants) had slaves, and our maidservants (descendants) had maidservants ..." At the end, it speaks to future generations: "...How I united the Turkic clans and tribes and ruled the state, I had carved and written here. So that some of you do not go astray and scatter again, I had it carved here as another warning... Read this and know it!" Sources interpret these lines as a warning against the cunning methods of the Tabgach, who drew distant peoples close with sweet words and soft silk, and then conquered them.

## What is Ikh Burkhant?

- **Location:** Dornod Province, Khalkhgol soum, 35 km north of Sumber, on the west bank of the Khalkh River.
- **Commissioned by:** Togtokhtur (To Van), Prince Ildenjun Bat-Ochir of the Setsen Khan aimag.
- **Built:** 1859–1864.
- **Main figure:** Avalokiteshvara, 28.74 m tall, with eight arms (more than 80 elbow-lengths).
- **Area:** About 2 hectares, with an outer enclosure of 220 × 97 m.
- **Restored:** 1994–1998.

## Who built Ikh Burkhant, and why?

Legend says the complex was built under the direction of [Togtokhtur](</about/history/people/togtokhtur-van>), Prince Ildenjun Bat-Ochir of the Khalkha Setsen Khan aimag, who became known across Mongolia as "To Van." Nomads, craftspeople, and blacksmiths built it through skill and the labor of ordinary people, overcoming many hardships. In the middle of the 19th century, repeated droughts and dzud (a harsh winter that kills livestock) struck the Setsen Khan aimag. Herds died in large numbers, and the aimag and its banners (khoshuu) faced poverty. Prince Togtokhtur consulted the nobles of the assembly but found no suitable solution. He then asked the senior lamas. They said that if an Avalokiteshvara were made in large numbers or on a large scale, the people would be saved from disaster. Researchers see several purposes behind the work:

- **Saving people from disaster.** Avalokiteshvara symbolizes protection from danger and misfortune.
- **Keeping wealth at home.** It is said that the aim was to stop the believers' great wealth from flowing to Tibet, China, and India, and to keep it in the local area.
- **Protecting the homeland.** It carried the meaning of guarding the nation's integrity and preventing the loss of Mongolia's eastern border to Manchu-Chinese rule.

## How was Ikh Burkhant built?

The nobles of the Setsen Khan assembly discussed at length where, when, and at what size to build the statue. The people of To Van's banner were skilled at hard labor, had experience building temples and monasteries, and had many craftsmen. So the assembly decided to build it in Prince Ildenjun's banner, with the whole aimag taking part. At first, the assembly agreed to make a standing copper statue 12 elbow-lengths tall. But To Van saw this as a wasteful use of great wealth. Instead, he decided to carve many figures and a stupa complex in relief from stone, on evenly sloping ground covering 2 hectares.

### Materials

Builders made the stone by binding crystal sand from Buir Lake, gravel from the Khalkh River, and white greasy clay from the Khamar pass. They added different colors with natural mineral pigments. The remains of the brick factory are at a place called Khuurt, near Baruun and Zuun Khalgait in Khalkhgol soum. Those of the pottery factory are southwest of Tsagaan Uul.

### Construction timeline

- **Spring 1859.** Nobles and lamas donated funds, and in the first month of spring, 40 people dug and leveled the site to a depth of 1–3 meters.
- **Stone.** Each stone was 45–50 cm long, 32–35 cm wide, and 20–25 cm thick. Hauling the stone is said to have taken the loads of 1,000 ox carts.
- **Craftsmen.** More than 180 craftsmen worked continuously, and each earned one tsen of silver per day.
- **Summer 1861.** The work was nearing completion when days of heavy rain damaged the ground and badly harmed the body of the statue. For this reason, the work was moved to its present site, a 35-degree slope in the bend of the Khalkh River's west bank.
- **1864.** A grand ceremony was held to consecrate the statue.

## What is the layout of Ikh Burkhant?

- **Two enclosures.** The stone enclosures are rectangular. The outer one measures 220 × 97 m, and the inner one measures 113 × 63 m.
- **Lantsa script.** Between the two enclosures, 20 Lantsa letters, each 165 × 110 cm, are set in horizontally.
- **Stupas.** Between the main statue and the inner enclosure, 12 stone stupas, each 200 × 110 cm, are depicted lying flat.
- **Prayer wheels.** Entering from the outer enclosure, you pass two stone prayer wheels, 5 m in diameter, set in relief.
- **Main figure.** At the center of the complex stands Avalokiteshvara, Savior from the Eight Dangers, on a sloping area of 30 × 26 m. The figure is 28.74 m tall, or more than 80 elbow-lengths.
- **Worship area.** In the stone frame beneath the statue's feet, a lotus is carved in relief. Next to it is a worship area of 44 × 26 m.
- **Stairs.** Stone steps 66 cm wide run along both sides of the statue. Pilgrims can walk around it clockwise without stepping on the figure.
- **Tara.** More than 20 figures of Tara surround Avalokiteshvara.

## What do the eight arms of Avalokiteshvara symbolize?

Avalokiteshvara sits with the right leg crossed and the left leg half bent. The eight arms are spread out, and a khadag (ceremonial silk scarf) and scarf-like sash wrap the body. Each arm is shown with a figure that represents one danger.

- **Right-side arms:** a lion, an elephant, a snake representing the danger of water spirits, and a fierce man.
- **Left-side arms:** a hook representing the danger of fire, water, and alcohol, and Garuda.

Most interesting of all, the figures around Avalokiteshvara look as if they were made from melted stone. Its exact secret has still not been solved. The craftsmen carved this hard black stone with the fine detail of silver work, which is remarkable.

## How has Ikh Burkhant been protected?

In 1949, the Soviet scientist A. P. Okladnikov surveyed the Khalkh River basin. He saw Ikh Burkhant, admired it, and guessed it was the work of a powerful state that once existed here. The scholar Kh. Perlee corrected this wrong conclusion and confirmed that To Van had it built in the middle of the 19th century. In the 1970s, some local people took stones from the statue for building foundations and gravestones, and the left foot was damaged. Between 1994 and 1998, the people of Khalkhgol soum in Dornod Province, the Institute of History and Philosophy of the Academy of Sciences, and the To Van Studies Society took the initiative to restore the complex. Lamas led by G. Divaasambuu read the Dashnyam Ravnai sutra for 36 hours. This allowed the monument to be passed on to future generations.

## Where does the name "Avtai Sain Khan" come from?

According to one legend, there was once a cruel custom of putting people to death when they grew old, at around 70. A young man did not kill his aging father and hid him instead. The khan shot a blessed arrow toward the pass and crossed it. The Tibetan lamas saw them and were afraid, and they released their dogs. But the dogs saw the khan's soldiers and began to fawn on them. Duut Lake lies in [Otgon soum](/province/zavkhan/otgon) in [Zavkhan Province (aimag)](/province/zavkhan). It is a lake with a legend that lives on in song. The story tells of the lake's spirit, a young man who loved to sing, and the bond between them. It was passed down through generations and later became the basis of the famous song "Duut Nuur".

## What is Duut Lake? Key facts

- **Location:** Otgon soum, Zavkhan Province.
- **Known for:** the legend of the lake's spirit.
- **Song:** "Duut Nuur." Composed by D. Tseveenravdan and N. Lutbayar, and sung by S. Batsukh, Honored Artist of Mongolia.

## How does the legend of Duut Lake begin?

Long ago, a handsome, charming boy grew up playing on the shore of Duut Lake. One day the lake's spirit appeared to people. Everyone ran away in fear. The boy, however, was curious. He waded into the lake to see the spirit and nearly drowned. The spirit saw that the little boy was not afraid of it, so it carried him to the shore and saved his life. After that, the boy kept coming back to play at the lake. The spirit secretly watched him every day and grew very attached to him. As the boy grew into a man, his fine build, handsome face and beautiful whistling and singing voice delighted the local people. The spirit loved him more and more. But it could not show itself to him, and it suffered. Every evening it wept and splashed the lake water.

## What happened when the young man left for military service?

One day the young man left his homeland to serve in the army. Soon people said the spirit had been seen around the lake. The locals were uneasy and could not understand why the fierce creature was appearing. In truth, the spirit was furious. It came out of the lake to look for the boy who no longer came. The young man finished his service but did not return for a long time. The spirit stopped appearing, and the locals' rumors faded into idle talk and were forgotten.

## What happened at the wedding feast?

One day the sound of a lively feast reached the spirit's ears. The spirit had never heard such a big celebration nearby, so it went to see and watched from a distance. There was the young man it had waited for so long. He was holding a wedding with a beautiful bride. With his familiar, lovely voice, he was singing sincerely to his beloved. The spirit could not bear it. It leaped into the feast and screamed until the ground shook. Then, with tears in its eyes, it looked at the young man and went back to the lake. The terrified people froze. They could not even go home and huddled together for the night. After that, no one went to the lake. Livestock that went to drink there never came back. The locals avoided the lake as a forbidden place, and eight years passed.

## How did the spirit take the boy?

The married couple had a son who imitated his father in everything. He was lively, clever and always on the move. One day the boy secretly went to the lake he had been forbidden to visit. His parents found out and rode after him in a panic. When they arrived, the carcasses of dead livestock were floating along the shore. A foul stench hung in the air, and the scene was terrifying. The mother wept and called her son's name. From deep in the lake came loud laughter, delighting in their grief. As the couple panicked, their horses and livestock stampeded away. The young man begged the spirit with tears: "Give me back my son." A voice rose from the depths of the lake: "Sing to comfort me now, as sincerely as you sang to your wife at your wedding feast." The young man sang with deep feeling and soothed the spirit. Moved by the song, the spirit said, "Come every day, sing to me and let me see you. Then I will give your son back." The young man knelt and swore to do everything it asked. The spirit returned his son.

## How did the spirit's anger calm down?

The three of them went home happy. Soon they packed up and moved away from the area. As promised, the young man was supposed to come. The spirit waited for a week. Its patience ran out. In a rage, it went to the family's home, but no one was left. They had all moved away. From that time on, the children of families who moved near the lake would not settle down, or they vanished without a trace. More and more people searched for their children, and the sound of crying could be heard all night. Every few days a child's cry could be heard from very close by. So parents could not leave the lake. They hoped they would meet their children again someday. A man from far away heard about this and made the journey. He asked about what had happened and went to the lake. Curious people followed him. They saw him sit on the shore and sing without stopping for six days. He sang beautifully, but no one understood why he sat there. Then, from the seventh day, the missing children appeared one by one, just as they had vanished, from out of nowhere. The people were overjoyed. They kept asking what had happened and forgot about the man who had been singing. It is said that after that, no one reported seeing the spirit again.

## How did the song "Duut Nuur" come about?

It is said that the song "Duut Nuur" came from this legend. It was composed by D. Tseveenravdan and N. Lutbayar and sung by S. Batsukh, Honored Artist of Mongolia. The song says that people sing at the lake and that the lake itself is moved to tears by singing. It uses images of deer, bare mountains and a lake like an eye. These express the sorrow of the legend and the unsettled feeling of hearing children cry. As the legend entered the song, the name of Duut Lake became even better known to the public.

The Danzanravjaa Museum is in Sainshand, the provincial center of [Dornogovi Province (aimag)](/province/dornogov). It preserves the legacy of D. Danzanravjaa, the Noyon Khutagt of the Gobi, a great Mongolian enlightener and poet. The museum was restored and officially opened on July 10, 1991. It moved into a new building in 2008. Learn more about [D. Danzanravjaa](</about/history/people/goviin-noen-khutagt-danzanravjaa>) and the city of [Sainshand](</province/dornogov/sainshand>).

![image](asset:tic-img-115d985bc3816b03)

## What is the Danzanravjaa Museum? Quick facts

- **Location:** Bag 2 (the smallest administrative unit), Sainshand, Dornogovi Province.
- **Opened:** July 10, 1991.
- **New building:** 2008.
- **Origins:** The "Exhibition Temple" of Khamar Monastery, and later the legacy of the Relic Temple.
- **Highlight:** 64 chests of items that G. Tudev hid and protected for 52 years.
- **Website:** ravjaamuseum.mn.

## How did the museum come about?

In the 1840s, Noyon Khutagt Luvsandanzanravjaa founded an exhibition temple. He filled it with his own works and crafts, gifts from his parents, relatives, nobles and devotees, and items he collected from across the land. He named it "Givaadin Ravjailin" and showed it to the public. People called it "Dingam" or "Nandin." After the khutagt passed away in 1856, his embalmed body and 1,500 chests of religious and cultural items were placed in this temple. It then became known as the [Relic Temple](</stories/news/sharilyn-sum>). Today's Danzanravjaa Museum is a revival of this "Exhibition Temple" of the former [Khamar Monastery](</destination/heritage/place/khamaryn-khiid>).

## What is the tradition of the keepers?

The disciple appointed to guard the Relic Temple came to be called the keeper. The khutagt's disciple Sh. Balchinchoijoo (monastic name Ishlodon) became the first keeper in 1856. He vowed to preserve his teacher's work. He served until the end of his life, then passed the duty to his son Gan-Ochir. The role passed from generation to generation:

- Sh. Balchinchoijoo (Ishlodon): the first keeper
- Gan-Ochir: keeper for 33 years
- Narya
- Gombo
- Tudev

According to the sources, the seventh-generation keeper, Z. Altangerel, has taken the vow. He works to cherish and restore the khutagt's legacy and to develop Ravjaa studies.

## How were the 64 chests saved?

In 1938, when Khamar Monastery was destroyed under Uvur Bayasgalant, a young lama, G. Tudev, slipped at night into the sealed temple. He carried the items out one by one. He saved 64 chests by burying them in pits dug in the countryside, covering them with sand, and hiding them in rock caves. They stayed hidden in the wild for 52 years and came to light in 1990. Only a small part of the 1,500 chests survived. Still, the items that keeper G. Tudev saved at the risk of his life are now displayed in the museum in Sainshand and at Khamar Monastery. Through the labor and wisdom of the khutagt's keepers across generations, they survived about 200 hard years. They are a priceless part of Mongolia's history and culture.

## What was the Children's Datsan?

At his school, the "Children's Datsan" (monastic college), the Noyon Khutagt taught boys and girls. There he trained professional artists for his play "The Story of the Moon Cuckoo":

- portrait performers, or actors,
- voice and speech cue-givers, or singers,
- blessing and praise reciters,
- hand and foot cue-givers, or dancers,
- jasachi, or managers,
- stage builders, craftsmen and attendants.

Graduates received a cloth certificate with a special seal. The museum keeps textbooks, drawings, printing blocks and stamps, related heirlooms and manuscripts from that time.

## What works did Danzanravjaa leave behind?

Here is an overview of the khutagt's creative legacy:

- more than 400 poems
- more than 100 long and short songs
- one volume of philosophical writing
- 10 notebooks of play scripts
- religious ritual works written in Mongolian and Tibetan
- dozens of drawings made by his own hand

Some of these are kept in the museum.

## Who has studied Danzanravjaa?

Many Mongolian and foreign scholars have studied the khutagt's life and work. They include the People's Writer Ts. Damdinsuren, Dr. D. Tsagaan, E. Oyun, D. Yondon, Academician L. Khurelbaatar, D. Tserensodnom, Dr. G. Lkhagvasuren and S. Khuvsgul. Foreign scholars include A. I. Gerasimovich and G. A. Uvarova from Russia, W. Heissig from Germany, N. Poppe from the United States and E. Gerelt from China. His works have been translated into English, German, Russian and Chinese.

- British scholar Simon Wickham-Smith translated a collection of his poetry into English.
- Researcher Pedro Zemaja translated the play "The Story of the Moon Cuckoo" into German.
- In China, his "Salvation in Three Essentials" hymn was released on CD in many thousands of copies. It is played in temples and monasteries such as Wutai and Gumbum.
- Robert Kastels, a citizen of Singapore, set his poem "Ichig Ichig" to music and released a CD.
- American journalist Michael Kohn published a book about the khutagt, "Lama of the Gobi."

## How did UNESCO honor Danzanravjaa?

In 2005, the 33rd UNESCO General Conference decided to mark the 150th anniversary of D. Danzanravjaa's passing on a wide scale. The anniversary was observed in 2006. In its official document, UNESCO described him as a Buddhist thinker. He was gifted in many fields, from music to architecture and from philosophy to theater. He was known abroad and had a deep influence on Mongolian culture and thought. UNESCO also noted that most of his works are being rediscovered alongside Mongolia's cultural revival.

The museum is in Bag 2 of Sainshand. Opening hours, ticket prices, photo fees and phone numbers may change. Please check the official website (ravjaamuseum.mn) before you go.

### When did the museum open?

It opened on July 10, 1991, and moved into a new building in 2008.

Of the 1,500 chests in the Relic Temple, the lama G. Tudev hid and saved 64 in 1938. They stayed hidden in the wild for 52 years and came to light in 1990. Today they are displayed in the museum and at Khamar Monastery.

The Bichigt rock paintings (full name: the Bayan-Airag Bichigt rock inscriptions) are 17 km from the center of [Biger soum](/province/gov-altai#biger) in [Govi-Altai Province (aimag)](/province/gov-altai). In the middle of Bichigt Gorge, several tall rocks are covered with images from bottom to top. Most of the paintings date from the Early, Middle and Late Bronze Age and the early Iron Age.

## Bichigt rock paintings: quick facts

- **Location:** Bichigt Gorge, 17 km from Biger soum center, Govi-Altai Province.
- **Sections:** three (Bichigt Rock 1, 2 and 3).
- **Researchers:** archaeologists D. Dorj and E. A. Novgorodova.
- **Period:** from the Early Bronze Age to the early Iron Age.
- **Notable images:** a chariot pulled by two horses, drive hunts, and a rider in a conical hat.

## How are the Bichigt rock paintings divided?

Archaeologists D. Dorj and E. A. Novgorodova studied the paintings in three sections.

- **Bichigt Rock 1** — deep in the gorge.
- **Bichigt Rock 2** — on the rocks of the next mountain.
- **Bichigt Rock 3** — on a mountain about 100 meters from the second section.

## What is depicted at Bichigt Rock 1?

This section is not very large, and it has few images. It shows Siberian ibex, argali, riders, an archer, and people driving livestock. On small rocks at the very top of the mountain, you can see a person on horseback, a tall and a short person leading one horse, an archer shooting at an argali with short horns and a stocky body and at an ibex, and a bowman hunting ibex. Next to them is a large, unidentified animal with a forked, antler-like shape at the level of its head. There are also images that seem later in date, showing riders and archers on foot shooting at each other. Between Bichigt Rock 1 and Rock 2, several boulders carry carved images. You can clearly see deer, ibex, a rider, a hunter aiming a bow, a strange creature, a rider dragging something like a thick tree root, two riders holding very large objects, and a person wearing a hat with two ears.

## Why is Bichigt Rock 2 the most important?

This section has the most images. Many are drawn over each other, which shows they were made at different times. For example, later archers and finely stylized ibex are layered over darkened images of deer and ibex. The clearer, later images show people on horseback holding bows. They wear tall, pointed hats with something like peacock feathers hanging down the back.

### Deer and riders

Deer are the most common images here. Some deer have sharp, upright antlers or many branching tines. Others are outlined with straight lines, which looks like a kind of tamga (clan mark). One unusual image shows a rider in a conical hat sitting sideways on a horse. The horse has long ears, gallops with bent legs, and wears a bridle and bit. This suggests a later date.

### The chariot image

The most interesting image is on the south side of the rock. Among many riders, ibex and archers, you can see a very faint, dark brown chariot and three ibex in the same color. A few meters from the chariot, a deer and a scene of deer hunting with a dog appear in the same color. The chariot is outlined with a fairly large box, two spokeless wheels, and an axle joining them. Two horses are harnessed to it. It is drawn as if seen from directly above, which makes it especially valuable for studying chariots. There are also a wheel with a cross in the middle and images of stocky horses with wide hooves. The latest image is a human head with dots between the eyes, a curled mustache and a small pointed hat.

## What hunting scenes are at Bichigt Rock 3?

The most interesting images in this section show animals being fenced in, chased and ambushed, known as drive hunts. There are three groups of images.

### First group

Ibex, argali, camels and a person are clearly distinguished. Near the drive hunt passage, a person holds a bow, there is a herd of horses, and a person has no weapon. On the other side, a herd of wild animals gallops while a person scares them forward. This is considered a depiction of the classic drive method that ancient hunters used.

### Second group

This shows a herd of ibex and argali running in one direction. There are no people or horses. Only lines show the drive itself, so researchers conclude that the image was meant to show a drive hunt.

### Third group

Inside three separate frames, some ibex and argali face one direction and others face another. In the upper corner of one frame are three strange figures: a rider in a hat with a feather stuck in it, an animal with a thick belly, and a figure that looks like a person at first glance and a bird at second. At the bottom of the frame are two long-bodied animals with their legs upturned. One has forked antlers like a deer. The other has long ears, a large mouth, big claws and a long, thick tail. Researchers such as archaeologist D. Dorj consider this a ritual image of a successful hunt.

## What period do the Bichigt rock paintings date from?

Most of the paintings are linked to the Early, Middle and Late Bronze Age and the early Iron Age. The layered images, bridled horses, chariot with two horses and people in pointed hats show that people left images in this gorge over many centuries. For other rock art in Mongolia, read pages such as [Arslant rock paintings](</destination/heritage/place/arslantyn-khadny-zurag>).

They show deer, ibex, argali, riders, archers, a chariot pulled by two horses, and drive hunts.

The Ulaanbaatar Railway (UBTZ) is one of the shortest routes in the Eurasian rail network linking Russia and China. [Ulaanbaatar](/province/ulaanbaatar) station is the hub of Mongolia's domestic and international rail, and its largest station. Mongolia's railway is part of the Trans-Siberian Railway and is also called the "Road of Friendship."

![image](asset:tic-img-4f7010575b4264ab)

## What are the key facts about the Ulaanbaatar Railway?

- **First line:** 1938, Ulaanbaatar–Nalaikh, 43 km, 750 mm narrow gauge.
- **Naushki–Ulaanbaatar:** 400 km, put into operation in 1949.
- **Ulaanbaatar–Zamyn-Uud:** 700 km, put into operation in 1955.
- **Ownership:** the joint Mongolian-Russian company "UBTZ."
- **Length:** 1,815 km including branch lines, with more than 80 stations and sidings. The main line linking Russia and China is 1,110 km.
- **Capacity:** 22–23 million tons of freight per year.

## How did Mongolia's railway begin?

Mongolia's railway history began with the need to supply the capital with coal. The first railway was laid in 1938 between Ulaanbaatar and Nalaikh. Steam trains ran on this 43 km, 750 mm narrow-gauge line and supplied the city's industry and residents with coal. Under a 1946 agreement between Mongolia and the Soviet Union, the broad-gauge main line was built. A plan for a joint company, UBTZ, was drafted in 1948. On June 6, 1949, the two governments signed an intergovernmental agreement. Prime Minister Marshal Kh. Choibalsan signed for Mongolia, and Ambassador Extraordinary and Plenipotentiary Yu. Prikhodov signed for the Soviet Union. On the morning of November 7, 1949, at 08:30, a ceremony was held to welcome the first train.

## When did direct international service open?

In 1952, the Soviet Union, Mongolia and China signed a trilateral agreement. On December 31, 1955, a ceremony opened the Ulaanbaatar–Zamyn-Uud railway. From January 1, 1956, the world was told that direct rail service had opened from Russia through Mongolia to China. As industry and construction grew, branch lines followed: Darkhan–Sharyn Gol, Salkhit–Erdenet, Bagakhangai–Baganuur, Airag–Bor-Undur, Sainshand–Zuunbayan, and Ereentsav–Bayantumen.

## What is the timeline of the Ulaanbaatar Railway?

### The 1950s and 1960s

- **1951.** A railway school and training complex was founded. In the 1953–1954 school year, a railway technical college opened. Between 1952 and 1957, about 3,500 people learned railway trades.
- **January 1, 1958.** The first issue of the railway workers' newspaper "Road of Friendship" was published.
- **1958.** The 13.7 km Khonkhor–Nalaikh line opened, and the 43 km narrow-gauge line of 1938 was closed in September.
- **November 4, 1963.** The 63.3 km Darkhan–Sharyn Gol branch line opened.
- **September 20, 1967.** A daily passenger train began running between Ulaanbaatar and Sukhbaatar, with Ulaanbaatar–Irkutsk passenger cars attached to it.

### The 1970s and 1980s

- **October 4, 1975.** The new Salkhit–Erdenet line opened.
- **May 30, 1976.** The Ulaanbaatar–Moscow passenger train "Narlag Mongol" began regular service and made its first trip.
- **1981.** Khutul station was built for loading and unloading. The 50 km Sainshand–Zuunbayan line, linked to an oil field, went into operation.
- **1982.** The 97 km Bagakhangai–Baganuur line.
- **1983.** The Ulaanbaatar–Choir–Baganuur passenger train began running.
- **1984.** The 20 km Tolgoit–Songino line.
- **1986.** The 112 km Mardai line.
- **1987.** The 120-bed Bayanburaal rest center for railway workers opened.

### The 1990s to the 2010s

- **1991.** R. Rash was elected head of the UBTZ administration, becoming the first Mongolian specialist to lead UBTZ.
- **October 4, 1993.** The international transport brokerage center was founded.
- **July 3, 1994.** A narrow-gauge line was laid between Zamyn-Uud and Erenhot (Erees). Chinese freight trains were received and transferred at Zamyn-Uud.
- **September 5, 1997.** The Khutul plant, able to produce 180,000 reinforced concrete sleepers a year, went into operation. This made it possible to strengthen track maintenance and increase transport capacity.
- **1999.** The local ticket distribution center opened, serving more than 8,000 passengers a day.
- **June 2004.** An underpass opened at Ulaanbaatar station.
- **May 2005.** The "Zagal 01" and "Zagal 02" electric locomotives, built by Mongolian engineers, began hauling freight.
- **November 11, 2008.** Zamyn-Uud-2 station was put into operation to increase the capacity of Zamyn-Uud station.
- **2010.** The longest train, with 80 wagons, was run.

As the mining sector grows rapidly, the railway needs more transport and throughput capacity. According to the source, freight volume grew 9–13 percent each year. In 2012, the railway carried 20.4 million tons of freight and 4.0 million passengers. At that time, UBTZ had 15,560 employees.

@@ FIELD seoTitle text
Gol Mod 2 Xiongnu Tomb: Shanyu Burial, Undur-Ulaan
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Gol Mod 2 is a Xiongnu shanyu tomb (AD 80–100) in Undur-Ulaan soum, Arkhangai, with a 47 m tomb, a Roman glass cup and chariots.
@@ FIELD region text
Arkhangai
@@ FIELD description richText
Gol Mod 2 is considered the largest aristocratic tomb among the royal tombs of the [Xiongnu (Hunnu)](</about/history/khunnu>) found in Mongolia. It lies in the Khanuy bag (the smallest administrative unit) of [Undur-Ulaan soum](/province/arkhangai#undur-ulaan) in [Arkhangai Province (aimag)](/province/arkhangai), at a place called Gol Mod on the Balgas steppe. More than 200 tombs, both main and satellite burials, stand here, and Gol Mod 2 is in the northeastern part of the group.

![image](asset:tic-img-97cc5574f0276ee6)

## What are the key facts about Gol Mod 2?

- **Location:** Arkhangai Province, Undur-Ulaan soum, Khanuy bag, Balgas steppe.
- **Date:** AD 80–100.
- **Size:** the main body is 47 m long, and the entrance passage to the tomb chamber is 38 m long.
- **Satellite tombs:** 29. They hold 28 people, from a child 1.7 years old to a 35-year-old man, buried along with one horse.
- **Sacrificial offering:** 21 horses were offered around the main tomb chamber.
- **Excavation leader:** Dr. Prof. D. Erdenebaatar (Ulaanbaatar University).

## Why is Gol Mod 2 so special?

This shanyu (Xiongnu ruler) tomb dates to AD 80–100 and caused a major stir in the history of Mongolian archaeology. No Xiongnu royal tomb found earlier in Mongolia had been this large. In structure, rituals, offerings and horse burial, it resembles other Xiongnu tombs. But its contents, their craftsmanship and their value are unmatched by any other tomb. Sadly, the large tomb seems to have been looted about 2,000 years ago.

## What was found in the tomb?

Horse gear, valuable items and chariots decorated with gold and silver were placed in the main tomb. The noble's coffin was adorned with embossed, stone-inlaid gold plates. This is linked to an ancient tradition of preparing a "rock house" for burial. All the Xiongnu royal tombs found so far have a wall-painted burial chamber. These include Gol Mod 2, Gol Mod 1, [Noyon Uul](</destination/heritage/place/noen-uulyn-bulsh>) and Duurlig Nars.

### The Roman glass cup

A glass cup found in Tomb 1 helped to date the burial. It was made by fusing and mixing two different kinds of glass. Such a cup was a luxury item in the courts of ancient Roman rulers and nobles. This glassmaking technique belongs to the second half of the 1st century AD and passed from ancient Greece to Rome. For this reason, the tomb was dated to AD 80–100. Cups like this are very rare in Eurasian archaeology. Three are kept in Germany and two in the United States, and the sixth was found in Mongolia. Besides dating the tomb, this find proves that the two ancient powers, Rome and the Xiongnu, had contact.

### The shanyu's remains

Only a skull fragment and a thigh bone survived from the shanyu's remains. This find is priceless for anthropology. About 100 Xiongnu anthropological finds exist, but remains of a king or noble had almost never been found. Researchers believe DNA studies of the ruling clan will help settle the debate over Xiongnu origins. A report published in the United States by the Russian scholar N. V. Polosmak, who studied the Noyon Uul tombs, shows how sensitive this issue is. She concluded that the Xiongnu were a tribe of Turkic and Persian origin. Because ancient Central Asian items turn up in the tombs, countries such as Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Turkey keep interpreting Xiongnu origins in their own ways.

![image](asset:tic-img-45d0cc033e7d376e)

### The chariots

Parts of 6–7 large and small chariots were found in the main tomb, along with fragments of other carriages. One chariot has Chinese characters and an elegant design, so the Han emperor may have presented it to the shanyu. All the other chariots are of Xiongnu make. Their remarkably fine work in gold, silver and bronze, and their stone inlays, show that luxury carriages were prized by the shanyus. Their design is extraordinarily artistic and rich in symbolic meaning.

### Marked shagai

In satellite Tomb 3, east of the great king's tomb, the burial of a child aged one year and seven months was found. The grave held more than 200 shagai (anklebone games), possibly the child's toys, and about 20 of them bear marks. Carved on the four sides of the anklebones are signs identical to the sun mark later used by Chinggis Khaan, the fork mark of Munkh Khan's line and the hook mark of Ogedei's line. This is of great interest.

## Who excavated Gol Mod 2?

Earlier, foreign scholars from Russia, the United States, France, Korea and other countries excavated and studied Xiongnu tombs. Gol Mod 2 was different. For the first time in their history, Mongolian researchers excavated and analyzed it on their own, without outside help, together with its satellite tombs and ritual structures. The work was sponsored by Tumen Khishigten LLC of the Genco group. The following people took part in the project:

- 6 research staff (archaeologists and anthropologists)
- more than 80 students of archaeology and history
- more than 80 military personnel
- more than 20 engineers, technicians, drivers and mechanics
- 4–5 people for logistics and the kitchen

This work made the skills of Mongolian researchers known to the world. Researchers from Ulaanbaatar University also newly identified early and middle Bronze Age cultural sites such as Chemurchek, Munkhkhairkhan and Tevsh. These finds may push the dating of Mongolia's Bronze Age history back by 1,000–1,500 years. To read more on this topic, see the article [Gol Mod 2 Is a Giant "Storehouse" of Xiongnu History](</stories/news/gol-mod-2-ni-khunnugiin-tuukhiin-avarga-aguulakh-yum>).

### How old is Gol Mod 2?

Based on the Roman glass cup, it has been dated as a shanyu tomb from AD 80–100.

Finds include a Roman glass cup, 6–7 chariots, gold plate decorations and shagai bearing signs identical to the marks of Chinggis Khaan's era.

Shoroon Bumbagar lies at a place called Ulaan Khereem in [Bayannuur soum](/province/bulgan/bayannuur) of [Bulgan Province (aimag)](/province/bulgan), 170 km west of Ulaanbaatar. In 2012, a joint team from the Institute of History of the Mongolian Academy of Sciences and the Institute of Turkic-Altai Studies of the Eurasian National University in Kazakhstan uncovered the fully intact tomb of an ancient nomadic aristocrat here. The tomb is about 1,500 years old. It is often called a "gallery" of ancient nomadic art.

![image](asset:tic-img-5e35c405d951795c)

## What is Shoroon Bumbagar? Key facts

- **Location:** Ulaan Khereem, Bayannuur soum, Bulgan Province. 170 km west of Ulaanbaatar.
- **Discovered:** 2012, by a joint Mongolian-Kazakh team.
- **Date:** 7th century, [the Turkic period](</about/history/turegiin-ezent-guren>).
- **Passage:** 42 m long, 1.8 m wide. The tomb chamber lies 7.5 m deep.
- **Finds:** About 550, including 134 gold items.
- **Special feature:** Never looted.

## What is the structure of the tomb?

More than a thousand years later, the tomb has not collapsed and is still sturdy. Inside, a person can walk freely. It has a stone ceiling, and the walls are built of flat stones. The entrance passage is 42 m long and 1.8 m wide. The tomb consists of a vestibule with four sections, two niches, and the main chamber. It slopes down underground to the chamber, 7.5 m deep. The main door was made of wood. On both sides, pits had been dug and covered with stones. The three inner doors were locked, and the key was hung on the last wooden door. The research team used that key to open the doors. Behind the door there was no building, only a pit chamber. A niche with a door was cut into the passage wall, and clay pots were found inside.

## What is painted on the passage walls?

The walls of the long passage carry more than 40 images:

- 24 human figures, each different from the others
- 2 animals with tiger bodies and dragon chests
- 7 branching trees
- 2 saddled and bridled horses
- 2 temple-like buildings
- a lotus flower
- the head of a cattle-like animal
- one dog
- 6 banners and flags

The depiction of the horses' manes shows how people of the Turkic period groomed their fine horses. Inside the tomb door, guardian figures stand facing the entrance. They have lion heads, snow leopard bodies, bird beaks, and tiger paws.

![image](asset:tic-img-fd8717c5b3b724d4)

## What was found at Shoroon Bumbagar?

About 550 items were found in total. Of these, 134 were gold items, 11 were clay items, and more than 30 were wooden items. The rest were made of silver, iron, and bronze.

### The coffin and gold items

A large wooden coffin lay not far from the guardian figures. Inside was a wooden box 80 cm long and 35 cm wide. In the upper part of the box were gold items wrapped in silk. In the lower part, the ashes of the deceased were placed in a silk bag. Beneath them, gold and silver coins were placed in another silk bag. The gold coins show human figures, scroll patterns, animals, ancient runic script, and Latin letters. To the left of the coffin, archaeologists found two wooden horses, a bridle, stirrups, and gold items that may be a crown. Most of the gold decoration has flower and cloud patterns.

### Clay figures

More than 300 figures and wood carvings were found:

- many clay figures of mounted soldiers in armor and helmets, some holding wind instruments;
- about 80 standing human figures with different clothing, faces, beards, and hair;
- a dog, pig, sheep, chicken, a winged creature called a garuda, frog, and horse;
- saddles, stirrups, bridles, and harness pieces.

The statue of the garuda is an especially unusual find. In the ancient tales of the East, it is a winged creature with a human chest and a bird's rump.

## Why is Shoroon Bumbagar important?

- **Rare completeness.** No tomb this complete and richly decorated had ever been found in Mongolia or Central Asia. Remains of such tombs were usually found east and south of Hohhot. This find extended their known range westward.
- **Unique nomadic art.** The site is dated to the 7th century, the Turkic period. Turkic and Uighur specialists from Japan and China visited and found that the imagery is quite different from the tombs of Tang officials of the eight ranks. They accepted that there is no basis for linking it to ancient Chinese art. So it is judged to be a work that reflects the aesthetic thinking, painting methods, and worldview of the nomads who lived in Mongolia.
- **Foreign relations.** Coins minted in present-day Istanbul in 610–630 were found, along with items linked to Byzantium, China, India, and Tibet. This shows that foreign relations were highly developed at the time, and that Western and Eastern cultures spread through Mongolian lands.
- **Never looted.** About 90–95 percent of the tombs found earlier had been looted. Here, excavation revealed traces that someone tried to rob the tomb long ago and failed. The person died buried under earth near the vestibule, together with his dog.

A tomb burial reflects a person's reputation and standing in society. Judging by the burial customs and the grave goods, the person buried here held a leading position in social and political life and was a great nomadic aristocrat.

## Who were the Khitans?

Chinese and foreign researchers consider the Khitans a people with the same origins and language as the Mongols. When they founded their state in Central Asia, they advanced the natural sciences and technology. They led their era especially in smelting and processing steel. Kherlen Bars is one example of their city planning.

## What was found at Kherlen Bars?

The ruins were first officially studied in 1953. The great wall was 1,600–1,800 m long, 4 m thick and 1.5–2 m high. Inside the wall and on its east side stood five-story and seven-story stupas. These stupas had an Indian style, and large images of the Buddha on their inner walls. Researchers excavated four buildings inside the wall, the foundations of small buildings near the smaller wall, and the remains of a lime kiln. They found the following:

- a large Buddha pedestal
- blue roof tiles
- bricks with and without scroll patterns
- clay sculptures
- fragments of finely made Buddha statues and decorative pieces
- figures of livestock
- arrowheads
- carved wood
- bone and metal items

These finds showed that the small building was a place of Buddhist worship.

## Was Kherlen Bars a military fortress?

Most of the finds relate to Buddhism. Even so, researchers have long believed the town was a military fortress and camp. Historical sources record that the Khitans stationed many military units, mostly made up of nomads, in the Kherlen River basin. They did this to put down uprisings by the tribes of the northwest. Scholars suggest that the tower among the ruins may have been a lookout post for watching the surrounding land from afar.

## What is the legend behind the name Kherlen Bars?

According to local elders, many centuries ago the Manchu emperor wanted to settle his son in Mongolia. He began building a town on the bank of the Kherlen River, in what is now Dornod Province. Local people heard about it and gathered in secret. They said, "Our beautiful land is about to fall to a foreign power." One wise man proposed a clever plan and suggested informing the lords of the banner (khoshuu). They decided to make an artificial tiger and create a great roar and commotion. As the emperor's party approached, the locals worked for many nights in a row. They hid in the willows along the Kherlen River and made the fake tiger roar. They drove many horses at a gallop and snapped willow branches. They made noises as if animals were fighting, and left tracks on the wet ground. Hearing this, the emperor ordered his son: "This land is beautiful, but it is full of beasts such as tigers and leopards. We cannot build a town here. Stop the work and return at once." The party fled and left the yard, wall and tower unfinished. The locals began to call the ruins "Kherlen's Tiger," both as a joke and with respect. Over the years the name changed slightly and became "Kherlen Bars." The legend is linked to the Manchu period. Keep in mind, however, that excavated finds show the town was built in the 10th century, in the Khitan era.

## What are the geographical features of the Tengis and Shishged River Basin?

The terrain is very distinctive. The mountains are relatively high and their slopes are mostly steep, so elevation changes sharply over short distances. In mountains with rocky crests, terraces have formed below the peaks, and several of them lie in layers. In general, this is a land of high ridges with rocky crests and river valleys between them. In terms of natural zoning, the area belongs to the West Khuvsgul subregion, which lies in the high-mountain and mountain taiga belt. The western part of Khuvsgul is the eastern end of the great Altai-Sayan geographic region, and the Tengis and Shishged basin represents the East Sayan region. As in the East Sayan Mountains, imposing mountains combine mountain tundra, meadow steppe and taiga here. Heavy snowfall, rocky scree, peaks, glacial lakes, upland bogs, fast rivers and deep gorge valleys form a unique natural mix. For this reason, the area differs from the Khentii taiga, the Orkhon-Selenge forests and even the eastern part of Khuvsgul. Most mountains do not exceed 3,000 m, but they rise high above their surroundings, and many have bare crests with rocky scree. As a result, the forest does not cover the land evenly. It grows only in belts. Mountains named Ulaan Taiga, Mungarag Taiga and Joshim Taiga are in reality a mix of rocky crests, lichen- and moss-covered mountain slopes and forest. In terms of ecosystems, mountain tundra covers the largest area. Next come mountain taiga, river meadows and high-mountain rocky crests.

This would help raise local people's incomes and increase national revenue. The source also stresses the need to bring "wild" travelers who roam freely into an organized system. That means setting tour routes and camping places and guiding travelers under the supervision of rangers, in line with the law.

The Dulaan Gobi Neolithic Site lies 50 km southwest of the center of [Erdene soum](/province/dornogov/erdene) in [Dornogovi Province (aimag)](/province/dornogov). This hollow is an ancient lake basin, ringed by hills and dotted with sand mounds and saxaul thickets. People lived here continuously from the Mesolithic through the Neolithic, the Bronze Age, and the medieval period.

## What is Dulaan Gobi? Quick facts

- **Location:** Dornogovi Province, 50 km southwest of the center of Erdene soum.
- **Discovered:** In 1955 by K. Yu. Efremov, an employee of the Ulaan Uul railway station.
- **Soil layers:** 12 layers containing archaeological and paleontological finds.
- **Period:** From the Mesolithic to the Middle Ages.
- **Finds:** Arrowheads, scrapers, flakes, cores, grinding stones, a bronze knife, and pottery.

## How was Dulaan Gobi discovered and studied?

In 1955, K. Yu. Efremov, an employee of the Ulaan Uul railway station, reported finding stone tools here. The site then entered Mongolia's archaeological records.

- **Kh. Perlee and N. Ser-Odjav.** They carried out the first survey. They concluded that people lived here in the middle and late Neolithic. In 1957 they published the finds under the name "Dulaan Us" (a well).
- **Ts. Dorjsuren.** Also in 1957, he added the site to the list of Stone Age sites in Mongolia under the name "Dulaan Gobi."
- **D. Dorj.** In 1971, in his work "Neolit Vostochnoi Mongolii," he described it as "Dulaan Us." He dated part of the finds to the 3rd millennium BC.
- **D. Tsevendorj and P. Khosbayar.** They carried out a joint archaeological and geological study.

## What is the geology of Dulaan Gobi?

The Dulaan Gobi hollow lies in the southeastern part of the Dornogovi uplift. It is bordered by the Tsant uplift to the northwest and the Adarman Mountain uplift to the southeast. To the southwest it joins the Zuunbayan hollow. The hollow is filled with red, mottled, and pale sediments of Mesozoic and Cenozoic continental origin. It holds 12 soil layers with archaeological and paleontological finds. They can be fully assigned to the Quaternary period. Two buried soil horizons, lake deposits, and river and foothill sediments are layered one above another. These layers preserve a unique ancient landscape that survived alternating warm and cold climate changes. Because they lie together with archaeological finds, they have great scientific value.

### Arrowheads

A total of 20 arrowheads were found:

- **Oval blade-type long points: 6.** Each is made from a narrow, long blade with one or two ridges. The tip and both edges were evenly retouched from the back. The cross-section is triangular or square. Points like these are dated to the end of the Mesolithic and the start of the Neolithic (Okladnikov, 1950).
- **Leaf-shaped points: 1.** It was shaped from both sides of a blade, and its cross-section is rhombic. Points like this are dated to the end of the 3rd millennium and the start of the 2nd millennium BC.
- **Triangular points: 13.**

### Other stone tools

- **Points: 2.** One is whole and the other is broken. A stone flake was evenly retouched on all sides.
- **Blades: more than 200.** Most are small blades.
- **Scrapers: 39.** There are 23 thumbnail-shaped, 6 side, and 10 corner scrapers.
- **Choppers: 2.** One edge of a flat flint stone was struck from two sides and from one side to form an edge.
- **Knives: 2.** One edge of a thin, flat flint stone was evenly retouched from both sides.
- **Ool-type tools: 2.** The side edge and tip were retouched from both sides. Tools like these are common in eastern Mongolia.
- **Spearhead inserts: 2.** These are triangular inserts made by shaping flint from both sides. They were thinned and flattened to fit into a shaft. By their make, they can be dated to the Mesolithic.
- **Cores: 62.** They include 41 Gobi-type cores and 14 prism-shaped cores.
- **Flakes.** They make up a large part of the collection.

## Is there evidence of farming at Dulaan Gobi?

Archaeologists found broken granite grinding stones, slab stones, and pestles used to process grain. This is evidence that farming developed to some extent in Mongolian lands from an early time.

## What Bronze Age finds are there at Dulaan Gobi?

A fragment of a bronze knife with a curved tip was found. Knives like this belong to a type widespread in Mongolia, southern Siberia, and Central Asia in the 12th–7th centuries BC (Kiselev, 1950; Volkov, 1967). To read about the bronze knife, see the article [Bronze knife](</stories/news/khurel-khutga>). Many fragments of clay pottery were also found. All these finds show that people lived continuously around Dulaan Gobi from the Mesolithic through the Neolithic, the Bronze Age, and the medieval period.

Tug Mountain and the Tug Temple are in Bulgan soum in [Dornod Province (aimag)](/province/dornod). They lie 75 km west of Choibalsan and 30 km from the Bulgan soum center. This ancient worship site, known as "Tug ovoo (sacred stone cairn)," is linked to the history of [Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan)](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>). In the past, the Tug Temple of Chinggis stood on the summit of Bayan-Erkht Mountain, on the south bank of the Kherlen River. ## Where is Tug Mountain, and what are the key facts?

- **Location:** Bulgan soum, Dornod Province. It is 75 km west of Choibalsan and 30 km from the soum center.
- **History:** It is said that Chinggis Khaan installed his military black banner here.
- **Tug Temple:** Founded in 1784. It may have been built by Prince Minjuurdorj.
- **Inside the temple:** It is said to have held statues of Chinggis Khaan and his nine generals.
- **Destroyed:** 1937.

## How is Tug Mountain linked to Chinggis Khaan?

It is said that Chinggis Khaan was returning from a battle with the Jurchen Altan state. He passed [Buir Lake](</destination/landscapes/buir-nuur>) and camped along the Kherlen River basin. His camp was on a flat terrace northwest of today's Tug Mountain. There he made an offering of food and tea. Then he installed his military black banner on the hilltop and worshiped it. People believe the Tug Temple of Chinggis Khaan was later built on this blessed place. The terrace where the great khan camped later became the meeting ground of the lords of Setsen Khan Province. The lords of the Khalkha Setsen Khan are said to have held their councils in a palace called the "Blue Ger" behind Tug Mountain. There is a story that in the 1940s some families built sheep pens from the felt of that ger.

## How was the Tug Temple built?

The Tug Temple was founded on Tug Mountain in 1784 in memory of this event. People believe Prince Minjuurdorj may have built it. It is said to have held statues of Chinggis Khaan and his nine generals. Archive records note that in the 11th year of the Tugeemel Elbegt era, or 1861, a blessing decree to "make a nordov" was issued to the "Tug-Chinggis Temple." "Nordov" means "to make treasures" in Tibetan. It referred to the ritual items used to worship the portrait image of Chinggis Khaan. During the harsh year of 1937, the temple was torn down. It is said that the large statue of Chinggis Khaan would not fit through the door. First they tried to separate the chest from the hips. In the end, they cut off the head to get it out. This suggests that the statue was probably made first, and the temple was built over it.

## What does the legend of Ulaan Chavgants say?

According to oral accounts, in the early 1800s a man arrived from far away to a place called Ulaan Ereg of Shiree, south of Kherlen Bars city. He seemed to have come from the southeast. He pulled an ox cart with seven red goats tied behind it. His name was Ulaan Chavgants, and he was a wise, quick-minded man who could read the land. He looked at Ulaan Ereg and the Shiree terrace and said, "This is a very important place. I will stay here for a week." He had a mild lung illness, so he took the waters of the Shiree mineral spring behind it. When he left, he is said to have told people: "Put three stones on this small mound. An ovoo will surely rise here. A large temple will soon rise on the small round hill in front."

## Where does the name Bayan-Erkht come from?

After Ulaan Chavgants, lamas came here and performed a water spirit ritual with a sacred vase. At that time, Tug Mountain was called "Bor Tolgoi," not "Bayan-Erkht." The monks who read the land saw it as the shape of a resting tiger, with Bor Tolgoi as the tiger's heart. The ancient Khitan people, who were of Mongol descent, founded a city here. They named it [Kherlen Bars City](</destination/heritage/place/kherlen-bars-khotyn-tuuri>), and people believe the name combined the river's name with the land's tiger shape. A family with three sons, said to be the family of Nasan Shuulen, had camped southwest of Bor Tolgoi. The monks met the middle son as he was herding lambs and asked his name. Without hesitation, the boy answered "Bayan-Erkht." The monks took the boy's quick wit and meaningful name as a good omen. They named Bor Tolgoi "Bayan-Erkht." People say the family of Nasan Shuulen then prospered, and their life flowed like a spring.

## What was the nature around Tug Mountain like?

Around the Tug Temple there were three small reed ponds, where many kinds of birds gathered. Red deer and roe deer would run in and stay for a few days. They would move about two stages (relay stations) south, settle and breed, and their numbers reached fifty or sixty. Wolves were common behind Tug Mountain, but they are fewer now. In old times people revered the wildlife here as an "animal paradise." It is said that during the shift to the market economy, the animals were killed off without mercy. So Tug Mountain and the Tug Temple of Chinggis, together with Ulaan Ereg, the Shiree terrace, the three small ponds, the animals and the birds, once formed a unique complex of nature, history and culture.

It was founded in 1784 in memory of the place where Chinggis Khaan's black banner was installed. It was torn down in 1937.

## What is Zaya Gegeen Monastery? Quick facts

- **Location:** Tsetserleg, Arkhangai Province, at the foot of Bulgan Mountain.
- **Founded:** 1631, in Chant Valley.

## How was Zaya Monastery founded?

Legend says that three great saints, Lama Gegeen, Zaya Gegeen and Shireet Gegeen, stayed here for the night. They agreed that the one whose bowl grew a flower would found a monastery on this spot. A flower bloomed in Zaya Gegeen's bowl, so the monastery was founded at the foot of Bulgan Mountain. The monastery split into an upper and a lower part, 5 km apart. It grew into a large settlement of 26 temples and halls, plus the monks' residences. More than 1,000 lamas held prayer services there. Mongolian craftsmen built the temples under famed master builders such as Dandarjamyan, Borchogba and Jigdel. In 1887, renowned master craftsmen Tugs Darkhan, Sonom, Luvsansharav and Buyant carried out repairs.

## Which hutagts lived at the monastery?

The following Zaya Pandita lamas lived at Zaya Monastery through the generations:

- The 2nd Zaya Pandita Luvsanperenlei: founder of the monastery and the first Zaya Pandita of Khalkha
- The 3rd Zaya Pandita Luvsanjigmeddorj
- The 4th Zaya Pandita Luvsannyandag
- The 5th Zaya Pandita Luvsanjigmednamjil
- The 6th Zaya Pandita Luvsantuvdenchoijinyam

## What is the history of the Guden temple?

The 2nd Zaya Pandita Luvsanperenlei made the Guden temple, built in 1696, his lavrin (residence). For this reason, the Guden temple was also called the Lavrin, or the Hutagt's Palace. There were two separate Gudens, west and east.

- **East Guden.** It held the remains of the 5th Bogd. Prayer services here honored Yamantaka (Yadam Sonomdoo) and Chogdog Burkhan.
- **Library.** The hutagt's library on the upper floor held ancient history, epics, literature and religious texts, sorted by Tibetan, Mongolian and Chinese language.

Like the Tsogchin (main assembly hall) of other monasteries, the Guden temples were surrounded by a wooden lattice fence. The tent-shaped roof of the upper floor stayed intact until 1940. During the political repressions, the mummified remains of the saints were taken out and buried in the ground. Later, the remains of Luvsanperenlei, the first Zaya Pandita of Khalkha, were returned to the monastery together with those of another saint.

### The fate of the Guden temple

- **Late 1930s.** It became the provincial fire brigade building, and the second floor of the central temple was taken down.
- **1941–1942.** It was handed over to a food processing plant and used as a vodka and general warehouse until the late 1950s.
- **From the 1960s.** It served as the provincial museum.
- **Since 1997.** It has been the provincial ethnography museum.

## What is the Semchin palace?

In 1802, the 3rd Zaya Pandita built the east and west Semchin in the Tibetan architectural style. They originally had three floors and a pavilion-style roof. The lower two floors were built of brick and the upper floor of wood, with a small room under the pavilion roof. In 1907, disciples of the 2nd Zaya Pandita from Inner Mongolia moved to Khalkha. In 1908–1909 they settled in the two Semchin. They took down the wooden room on the third floor and made it a prayer hall. They turned one Semchin into the Tsogchin Khas temple. Repairs in 1909–1910 gave the buildings their present look.

### East Semchin

The front had 16 columns, with two doors between each pair of columns. In the 1820s the 5th Zaya Pandita held the Kalachakra (Duinkhor) prayer service here. After the saint moved to the lower monastery, the services stopped. The building became a storeroom for items used in Zaya Pandita celebrations, such as the datsar tukher, the juuz carriage and the damnuurga. In the 1930s it became a garage for the fire engine.

### West Semchin

Its exterior matches the East Semchin, but the interior layout differs. The upper floor has three rooms. The west wall of the middle room held a double bed with a brown sandalwood screen, four sandalwood cabinets, three sandalwood tables, 50 sandalwood chairs inlaid with stones, and the Dalai Lama's bed. The lower floor also has three rooms. In the middle of the west wall of the middle room stands the sandalwood chair where the Saint sat. In the first years of the People's Revolution, taxes were levied on temples and monasteries. The furniture was sold off and lost.

Portraits of Buddhas are painted on the slope of Bulgan Mountain behind the monastery. It is said that they become clearer each time rain washes over them. During the political repressions, people tried to remove them but failed. Researchers have still not worked out the makeup of the monastery's blue bricks or of the paint used for the Buddhas.

## What are the key facts about Khuvsgul Lake?

- **Location:** Alag-Erdene, [Renchinlkhumbe](</province/khuvsgul/renchinlkhumbe>), Khankh and Chandman-Undur soums in Khuvsgul Province. It is 101 km north of Murun.

## What makes Khuvsgul Lake special?

Khuvsgul Lake formed about 7 million years ago. According to published sources, it ranks second in Asia for its volume of water and holds 1 percent of the world's fresh water. About 70 percent of the lake bottom is deeper than 100 meters. Water clarity reaches 24.5 meters. Twelve species of fish live in the lake, including Siberian grayling and lenok. The clear, fresh water is the lake's greatest treasure. The lake freezes completely in the second half of November, and the ice reaches 1 meter thick. Majestic mountains surround the lake. To the southwest rise the Khoridol Saridag and Bayan ranges, 3,000–3,200 m high. The west shore is steep and rocky, while the east shore is gently sloping. West of the lake lies the [Darkhad Depression](</destination/landscapes/darkhadyn-khotgor>), one of the coldest places in Mongolia. - **Modon Khui.** It has especially beautiful scenery.
- **Baga Khui.**

## What wildlife lives in the Khuvsgul Lake basin?

In the Khuvsgul Lake basin, 59 mammal species, 244 bird species and 750 plant species have been recorded. About 60 of the plant species are medicinal plants.

### Mammals

The forests and taiga are home to sable, Siberian weasel, raccoon dog, lynx, musk deer, moose, brown bear, wild boar, roe deer, red deer, squirrel, stone marten, steppe polecat, Pallas's cat, corsac fox, snow leopard, fox, wolf, reindeer, argali and Siberian ibex.

### Forests

Forest covers 28 percent of the basin, or 255,420 hectares. Trees include larch, broadleaf birch, fragrant poplar, Siberian spruce, Siberian larch, Scots pine, Siberian stone pine and Siberian fir.

## Who lives in the taiga around Khuvsgul Lake?

The Tsaatan (Dukha) reindeer herders live in the Khuvsgul taiga. They call themselves Uighur people. Their native homeland is the Mongol Sharyn Pass and the Shishged River. Historians believe they migrated from Tuva, settled in the Khuvsgul taiga and became Mongolized people of Uriankhai descent. The Tsaatan are a national minority with their own language, customs, beliefs and a unique way of life. They practice shamanism. They herd reindeer, use their products and hunt, and they move through all four seasons following the reindeer pastures. They live in temperatures of −31 to −50 °C. According to researchers, the Tsaatan have no written history, and their traditional customs are fading. Reindeer numbers are shrinking, and the animals suffer from disease and stunted growth. This is hurting the Tsaatan's living standards and traditional way of life. The [Darkhad](</about/culture/ethnic-groups/darkhad>) ethnic group also lives in this area.

## Why is Khuvsgul called a "sea"?

When Mongolia was under Qing rule, people were burdened by taxes and levies. The Manchu emperor decided to tax even the lake. To avoid the tax, locals argued, "Khuvsgul is not a lake but a sea." When asked how they could prove it was a sea, they answered, "Because 100 rivers flow into Khuvsgul." Manchu and Chinese experts had studied the lake well and knew that 100 rivers did not flow into it. So a day was set to count the inflowing rivers. Before that day, the Mongols made offerings to the mountains and waters, lit offerings and had sutras read to appease them. Then a steady rain fell. Every mountain gully and channel became a stream, and it is said that in just one section the count passed 100, so the counting could go no further. When this was reported to the Manchu emperor, he is said to have accepted Khuvsgul as a sea and exempted the people from the tax.

## What are the key facts about Amarbayasgalant Monastery?

- **Location:** Selenge Province, [Baruunburen soum](</province/selenge/baruunburen>), at the foot of Burenkhaan Mountain, at the source of the Iven River.
- **Imperial order:** In 1725, the Manchu (Qing) Emperor Enkh-Amgalan (Kangxi) ordered it built and paid 100,000 lan of silver from the state treasury.

## How was Amarbayasgalant Monastery built?

In 1725, the Manchu (Qing) Emperor Enkh-Amgalan issued an order to build a monastery for Zanabazar and paid 100,000 lan of silver from the state treasury. In 1726, many Mongolian master craftsmen began work. They built it with brick and wood and finished in 1737. By order of Emperor Enkh-Amgalan, the craftsmen who took part received titles such as "Gifted Master," "Exceptionally Brave Master," "Diligent Carpenter," "Sharp Master," "Supreme Artisan," "Skilled Carpenter," and "Meritorious Master." They were also rewarded with lan of silver and sheep.

## Where does the name Amarbayasgalant come from?

The monastery's name is a Mongolian name. Lamas searching for a site for the monastery met a boy and a girl playing at building a ger out of stones on the spot where the monastery now stands. The children were named Amar and Bayasgalant, so the monastery was named after them.

## What was the monastery's layout?

At its height, the monastery was a major religious center with 10 monastic colleges, more than 50 temples, and more than 6,000 lamas and monks. Inside a fortress wall measuring 175×207 m, more than 40 temples were built. They included the Temple of Buddha, the temples of Ayush, Makhgal, Maidar, and Narkhajid, the palace of the Bogd Gegeen, and the tomb of Undur Gegeen. Of these, 28 remain today. The monastery now has the Yanpai Gate, the Makhranz Temple, the History Temple, the Treasury Boipor, the Tsogchin Temple, the Lavrin Temple, and the Shar Ordoo (Yellow Palace), known as the hearth of Mongolia. The monastery once kept the remains of Zanabazar's body, as well as those of the Third and Fourth Bogds.

### Tsogchin Temple

Tsogchin Temple is a two-story building with a 32×32 m footprint. It is regarded as a unique masterpiece of Mongolian architecture for its craftsmanship and decoration. Its four large main pillars are grooved down the middle, which carries rain and snowmelt from the roof away from the building.

### Mongolian texts of the Ganjuur and Danjuur

The monastery holds Mongolian texts of 108 volumes of the Ganjuur and 226 volumes of the Danjuur. They contain more than 1,260 chapters on ancient philosophy, medicine, geography, art studies, music, and astronomy.

## What happened to the monastery during the repressions?

The 1937–1938 repressions struck the monastery, and hundreds of lamas were persecuted. Rare and precious statues, sacred objects, and sutras collected over more than 200 years were destroyed beyond restoration. In 1943, the monastery was placed under state protection and restored.

## What is the Gongor stupa?

At Amarbayasgalant Monastery, people perform the worship of the stupa of [Gongor Burkhan](/about/culture/religion/gongor-burkhan). The Gongor stupa ceremony is held every year in August. These stupas are made only at this monastery, and they are consecrated under very strict rules.

- **Contents.** Nine gems, rare medicinal plants, juniper incense, tea, and other sacred items are placed inside.
- **Worship.** The person who commissioned the stupa brings it to this monastery once a year to be worshipped.
- **Care.** The stupa must be kept free of dirt, and it must not be moved or handled carelessly.
- **Material.** Gongor is a white deity, so many people commission the stupa in pure silver.

The Gongor stupa is called the "stupa of prosperity." It is believed that commissioning one brings blessings and increases wealth.

## What is the legend of Jarun Khashor Stupa?

Near the monastery stands a large stupa called [Jarun Khashor](</destination/heritage/place/jarun-khashor-suvarga>), also known as "Slip of the Tongue." It was initiated by local residents of Baruunburen soum, O. Badam and D. Pagvajav. According to legend, a generous old woman asked the khan for permission to build a stupa to earn merit, and the khan told her, "Do as you wish." With her four sons and one servant, she hauled earth and stones by elephant and donkey and made good progress. Then the local lords decided that a poor woman building a stupa would harm the reputation of the prime minister, the wealthy, and other powerful people, and asked the khan to stop the work. It is said that the khan replied, "A khan keeps his word. Since I have already spoken, I will not go back on it." The stupa thus came to be called "Slip of the Tongue," or Jarun Khashor. It is also called the Eye Stupa. It is said that pilgrims who gaze at the eyes of the stupa are cleansed of sins and misdeeds. Around the stupa, 108 prayer wheels were installed with donations from devotees, and each wheel bears a raised image of the Soyombo symbol created by Zanabazar. Behind the stupa, a 13-meter-tall figure of [Bogd Zonkhov](/about/culture/religion/bogd-zonkhov) was made with two disciples, Jaltsavjii and Khaidavjii. A path of 108 steps leads up to Bogd Zonkhov, with three prayer wheels to turn along the way where you can catch your breath.

The Budaryn Chuluu petroglyphs are located more than 70 km southwest of the center of Ongon soum in [Sukhbaatar Province](</province/sukhbaatar>). Locals call the site "Dogshin Budaryn Chuluu." It has unusual rock formations, and on two sections of its rock wall, figures of people, horses, livestock and wild animals are pecked across the whole surface. The carved panel is 8 m 70 cm long.

![image](asset:tic-img-2a9e35b60067a83b)

## What are the key facts about Budaryn Chuluu?

- **Location:** More than 70 km southwest of the center of Ongon soum, Sukhbaatar Province.
- **Discovered:** 1996, by the Mongolian-Korean "Eastern Mongolia" project.
- **Panel size:** 8.7 m long, in two sections.
- **Technique:** pecked across the whole surface.
- **Period:** from the end of the 2nd millennium BCE to the first half of the 1st millennium BCE.
- **Images:** people, horses, dogs, gazelles, roe deer, wolves, ibex, snakes.

## Why are the Budaryn Chuluu petroglyphs important?

Before a field team of the joint Mongolian-Korean "Eastern Mongolia" project found this site in 1996, no rock paintings or petroglyphs had been found in Sukhbaatar or Dornod provinces. So Budaryn Chuluu extended the known range of rock art in Mongolia to its far east. It is also unusual in its themes, content and technique. The images on one panel are not a random collection. They tell a story. They clearly try to show relations among members of a clan and scenes from daily life. They also show the relationship between people, animals and nature.

## How are people and horses depicted in the carvings?

People and animals are shown fairly realistically, and a person is drawn large in the middle of the two panels. This is one of the main themes of Bronze Age rock art. In late Bronze Age rock art, when the horse came to be valued as a mount, people are shown leading or riding horses, as they are here. Horse bones are the most common animal bones found in Bronze Age graves excavated in eastern Mongolia, which is seen as a sign of the respect given to horses. The carvings do not show the wild horses of the Paleolithic, with their big bellies and short legs. They show led horses instead, which helps date the images. Human figures are the most numerous, and all of them express some kind of action. People are drawn in outline in the traditional way. They lead horses and dogs, hold hands, chase each other, wrestle or lie one on top of another. Early ocher paintings from the Stone Age do not include human figures.

### A person leading a dog

The image of a person leading a dog shows that, of all wild animals, humans domesticated the dog first and made it a loyal helper and companion. In Bronze Age petroglyphs, dogs are mostly shown with a person during a hunt.

## What does the ladder-like line mean?

In the middle of the first panel there is a vertical line with crossbars, like a ladder. This is a rare and interesting motif. Similar images appear elsewhere:

- In Bronze Age ocher paintings at Ikh Tenger and Gachuurtyn Am near Ulaanbaatar, two or four lines drawn beneath people and horses depict a road.
- In Bayanzurkh soum, Khuvsgul Province, a pair of horizontal lines with crossbars was drawn in ocher.
- At the Bichigt rock in Bugat soum, Bulgan Province, a red ocher painting shows two vertical lines, as at Budaryn Chuluu, with the lower ends closed.

Researchers date such ocher paintings to the 2nd–1st centuries BCE. No similar image has been found among petroglyphs so far.

## What animals are shown?

Wild animals are another main theme at Budaryn Chuluu. They are the animals most common in the area: gazelles, roe deer, wolves and ibex.

- **Ibex.** They are shown standing on four legs, with the legs not overlapping and the tail drawn long and curved. They closely resemble the rock art at Baatar Khairkhan in Khovd, Tevsh Mountain in Uvurkhangai, Del Mountain in Dornogovi and [Aravjikh](</destination/heritage/place/aravjikhyn-khadny-zurag>) in Umnugovi.
- **Gazelles and wolves.** They are drawn very neatly and clearly. They stand facing livestock and people, or flee in fright.
- **Snakes.** Snakes are common in rock art. Ancient people linked the snake with both good and evil. In the legends of some peoples in Asia and the Americas, the snake is a sign of disaster, while others honor it as a spirit of the land and water. A snake is among the nine spirits of the faith, which may be linked to ancient shamanic tradition.

## How old are the Budaryn Chuluu petroglyphs?

Researchers compared the technique, style and content of the carvings with other monuments in Mongolia. They date the Budaryn Chuluu petroglyphs from the end of the 2nd millennium BCE to the first half of the 1st millennium BCE.

## What is Gelenkhuu's Stupa? Key facts

- **Location:** Khuvsgul Province, 10 km north of the center of Jargalant soum, Khetsuugiin Khutul.
- **Built by:** Kh. Gelenkhuu.
- **Size:** the base covers 2.5 m², and the stupa is 3 m tall including the dome and the bell.
- **Material:** dressed and flattened reddish-yellow granite, sealed with clay.
- **Distinctive feature:** the opening deliberately faces north.
- **Type:** a stupa built as a personal memorial.

## What is the stupa's structure?

It is said that Gelenkhuu gathered the materials for the stupa himself and made the fine blacksmith's tools for the work with his own hands. The base covers 2.5 m², and the stupa is 3 m tall including the dome and the bell. He dressed the reddish-yellow granite, shaped it into flat squared blocks, stacked them and sealed the gaps with clay. The opening deliberately faces north, which may reflect the feelings of the man who built it. A stupa is a structure built to hold sacred relics and Buddhist objects, or to mark a historical event. Gelenkhuu's Stupa is a personal memorial stupa.

## Who was Gelenkhuu?

In brief, Kh. Gelenkhuu was born in the late 1880s, the son of Khainzan, a poor commoner in the banner (khoshuu) of Dalai Choinkhor Wang in Sain Noyon Khan Province. From childhood he was bright and lively, curious about everything and always dreaming. That made his life story read like a legend. As a child, at his parents' wish, he became a novice at Agt Monastery and later at Mandakh Monastery. But he fled monastic life and roamed the mountains hunting. He was known at home as a clever, troublesome boy, so talk quickly spread that "Gelenkhuu ran away from the monastery." In his home area of Devuur, he won wide respect for introducing marmots, for damming the river to dig irrigation ditches and grow vegetables and fruit, and for his metalwork. Because of this skill, it is said he was invited, together with renowned Khalkha craftsmen, to help cast the copper "Jadamba" statue for the 8th Bogd Javzandamba.

## How did Gelenkhuu "fly"?

After he first saw an airplane, Gelenkhuu began to watch birds in the sky. He found a bird's wing and took it apart feather by feather and put it back together. Nobody knew about this except his wife Norov and his friend Tseren. He collected thin, supple hide, glue and eagle wings, and made something in secret in a cave. One day Gelenkhuu strapped on the wings he had made and leaped from Dunter Cliff. He spread his artificial wings, held out both arms, and landed among a flock of sheep gathered below the cliff. For a safe landing, he had asked his daughter to round up sheep and wait below the cliff. Later he told people that, in the air, it felt as if the cool air was pulling him up and lifting him. The story of his leap from the cliff spread quickly across the country like a legend, and Gelenkhuu became known as Mongolia's first "pilot." In Murun, the provincial center of Khuvsgul, a statue honors him in his winged form.

## What else did Gelenkhuu build?

- **A canal.** He dug a canal to link the Ider River and the Devuur stream, until it reached the sheer rock wall of Baga Khetsuu. Gelenkhuu noticed that rock expands in heat and shrinks in cold. He lit a fire at the foot of the wall, then suddenly splashed cold water on it. The rock cracked, and he repeated this until he had opened a large cave.
- **Guns and knives.** He forged guns, knives and flint strikers, and sold them to nearby families. He fitted a simple flintlock gun with a mechanism that let it fire twice, which amazed people. Once, a local young man examining his gun accidentally fired it and injured Gelenkhuu's wife. Even so, Gelenkhuu did not give up his craft.
- **Shamanic knowledge.** Led by his curiosity, he became a shaman's apprentice and traveled around the banner learning to practice shamanism. But he decided "there is no magic in it, it is the work of lazy people," and he left his teacher.
- **Farming.** He tried to introduce marmots in Devuur. He branched a canal off the river to bring water to his fields, planted grain and vegetables, and for a time worked as a farmer and vegetable grower.

## How was the stupa built?

One day, Gelenkhuu hitched up an ox cart, hauled clay and stones to Khetsuugiin Khutul, and began piling them up. "I have committed many sins, and now I will repent," he said, and that is how the stupa came to be built. Before long, Khetsuugiin Khutul became a busy place, and the six syllables of the mani mantra were added to the stupa. Today it is still called "Gelenkhuu's Stupa."

## How did Khugnu Tarna become a protected area?

The area lies in the southern part of the small mountains of the Orkhon-Selenge basin. In 1997, it was given protection as a nature reserve to preserve the unique mix of taiga and steppe plants and animals around Khugnu Khan Mountain. Later, more people and livestock moved through the area. Plants and pastures were worn down, trees and berries were picked without restraint, and people began illegally cutting the Khugnu Tarna larch. So in 2003, State Great Khural Resolution No. 30 expanded the protected area around the Tarna River, the [Mongol Sand Dunes](</destination/protected/mongol-els>), and the Ikh and Baga Mongol mountains. It became a national park to help develop organized tourism.

## What is the geography of Khugnu Tarna like?

By natural zone, the area belongs to the Khan Khentii Mountain zone. Its highest peak is Tsetserleg, at 1,968.8 m above sea level.

- **Rocks.** Ancient sandstone, metamorphic shale, argillite, granite, marble and very thick acidic volcanic rock dominate. Khugnu Khan Mountain is mostly made of light gray granite.
- **Minerals.** Rock crystal and smoky quartz, topaz, black, white and pink mica, magnetite, tungsten, agates of many colors and fluorite are plentiful.
- **Water.** There are almost no rivers around Khugnu Khan Mountain. Only the Tarna River flows past the Mongol Sand Dunes along the western foot of the mountain. It begins in the Khangai Range. The Tarna River joins the Tuul River from the east, so it belongs to the Orkhon-Tuul basin. Khugnu Khan Mountain has several springs with good groundwater supplies.

The strip of sand that runs 80 km to the southeast, east of the Tarna River, is called the Ikh Mongol Sand Dunes. It is widest, at 9–10 km, near the Sharlin and Jargalant river junction, the source of the Tarna River, and near Ikh Mongol Mountain. Plants partly anchor the sand, so it moved relatively little in the past. In recent years, however, overgrazing has caused the sand to shift.

Woody plants such as elm and caragana grow among the rocks, scree and gullies of the mountain.

Legend says that when the Oirat and Khalkha were in conflict, the Oirat ruler Galdan Khan went looking for the Khalkha leader Undur Gegeen Zanabazar around this mountain. The Bichigtiin Am rock art is in the area of [Bayanlig soum](/province/bayankhongor#bayanlig) in [Bayankhongor Province (aimag)](/province/bayankhongor), in the Bichigtiin Am gorge of Ikh Bayan Mountain. ## What is Bichigtiin Am? Key facts

- **Location:** Bayankhongor Province, Bayanlig soum, Ikh Bayan Mountain.
- **Period:** from the Bronze Age, around 3000 BC, to the 8th century AD.
- **Technique:** pecked into the rock.
- **Most common image:** Siberian ibex, followed by red deer.
- **Main themes:** hunting, carts, plows and battles with bows and arrows.

## How old are the Bichigtiin Am rock paintings?

In ancient times, the natural and climatic conditions around Bichigtiin Am were clearly better for human settlement than they are today. For many periods, from at least the Bronze Age, around 3000 BC, to the early medieval period, the 8th century AD, people kept adding new images to the rocks of this gorge.

![image](asset:tic-img-b0028ea4beb45d91)

## Which animals are shown?

### Ibex and hunting

The most common animal is the Siberian ibex, followed by red deer. They are shown singly, in pairs and in whole herds. Many images show hunters shooting ibex and deer with bows and arrows, alone or in groups. They show how important hunting was in people's lives at the time.

### Deer

Today, ibex still live in herds of dozens in the spurs of the Govi-Altai Range. In ancient times, however, scholars wonder whether this area was moist enough, with forests and plenty of grass, to support deer. Or, deer images may be common here because the deer was honored among the nomads of Khangai, hundreds of kilometers away, and held a respected place in their art, and this imagery spread to the Gobi. The deer are drawn in a highly stylized way, with a long, narrow bird-like beak, long antlers with many branches like a spiral pattern, and a slender, long body. In the study of ancient art in Asia and Europe, this method is called the "animal style." It appeared in Mongolia and Central Asia in the Bronze Age and spread across the steppe zone of Asia and Europe in the early Iron Age.

### Predators and snakes

Many images show predators such as wolves, snow leopards and lynxes chasing argali, ibex and deer, surrounding them and trying to catch and eat them. The predators are drawn with open mouths and bared teeth, which is a remarkable feature of the art. Images of coiled, twisting snakes are also common. In the worldview of the ancient nomads of Central Asia, the snake was a creature of the underworld. People feared it and tried to appease and honor it, and these images reflect that.

## What do the livestock and farming images show?

Another important theme at Bichigtiin Am is animal herding. Many images show people leading horses, cattle and camels, and hunting on horseback or camelback. Some show people riding horses with and without saddles.

- **Horse-drawn carts.** One image shows a person driving a cart. Inventing the cart and using animals to pull it was a huge advance in the history of human transport.
- **Plows.** One image shows cattle yoked to a plow, with one person leading them and another guiding the plow behind to till the ground. It is a very rare find in the Mongolian Gobi connected with farming.

## How are battles with bows and arrows shown?

Many images show riders and people on foot fighting with bows and arrows. The bow and arrow is a long-range weapon that ancient people made in the Middle Stone Age, using the springiness of wood. Until firearms spread in the 16th century, it was humanity's strongest long-range weapon for tens of thousands of years. Two types of bow are clearly visible in the Bichigtiin Am images: the simple bow and the composite bow. The composite bow is reinforced with horn and sinew, so it shoots farther and is more durable.

## Why did ancient people draw on rock?

When ancient people carved images on rock, they expressed their wish for a successful hunt and victory over enemies. They also carved many images on the theme of fertility, as symbols of the multiplying of game animals and of the peaceful growth of their children, relatives and clans.

## Why are the Bichigtiin Am rock paintings important?

In size, themes, composition, technique and all artistic features, the Bichigtiin Am rock art is a major site of great scholarly importance. This is true not only in Mongolia but also among the rock art of Southern Siberia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan and northwest China, where petroglyphs are widespread. To read about a nearby ancient site, see the [Tsagaan Cave](</destination/landscapes/tsagaan-agui>) page.

### Where are the Bichigtiin Am rock paintings?

They are in the area of Bayanlig soum, Bayankhongor Province, in the Bichigtiin Am gorge of Ikh Bayan Mountain.

### Which images are most common at Bichigtiin Am?

Ibex images are the most common, followed by deer. The rock art also shows predators, snakes, riders, carts and plows.

### How old are the Bichigtiin Am rock paintings?

People kept adding carvings over many periods, from the Bronze Age, around 3000 BC, to the 8th century AD.

## How was Manzushir Monastery founded?

Donkhor Khutagt Luvsanjambaldanzan was the incarnation of the first Manzushir lama. He led the building of the monastery's first temple on the slope of Bogd Khan Mountain in 1733. Many more temples followed:

- **1736.** Tsanid Temple
- **1760.** Badmayogo Temple
- **1770.** The Bogd's palace, called Shar Khuree
- **1780.** Jud Datsan
- **1790.** Gombo Temple
- **1867.** Mamba Datsan

The Khangal, Ergedeg Maidar, Suvargan, Seruun Lavrin and Mangal temples were also built with remarkable architectural skill. They blended with the beautiful nature of Bogd Khan Mountain and became known as "the Arutai of Khalkha Mongolia." In the late 18th century, the Tsogchin Temple was built. When the monastery was destroyed in 1937, it had a history of more than 200 years and 21 buildings. Lamas were arrested and their property was seized. Tserendorj held the gavj and agramba degrees and was a learned lama. Together with the 8th Bogd Javzandamba Khutagt, he took an active part in the national liberation revolution of 1911. In 1937, he was accused in a false case known as the case of Yonzon Khamba Luvsankhaimchig and was executed.

The monastery ruins and the large Buddha images carved into the rocks on the mountain slope remain as rare historical and cultural monuments. For this reason, they were placed under state protection in 1998.

## How did people honor Bogd Khan Mountain?

The people of Khuree (the old name of Ulaanbaatar) have long honored Bogd Khan Mountain as "the unmoving center, the never-ending sacred spring, the never-fading beautiful flower." The old man said, "Then find the place where I set my foundation." Manzushir lama threw his silver ladle upward. It came down through the hole of a gold coin lying on the ground and stood upright. Seeing this, Tulgat Old Man accepted him, saying, "Manzushir lama, you are indeed the owner of this land." So it is said.

## Who were the Tumen Khishigten?

The Tumen Khishigten were Chinggis Khaan's closest troops, selected in every respect. The Secret History of the Mongols records his order this way: "From the ninety-five thousand I have set apart my Tumen Khishigten. Care for them well, and think of them as the sons who will sit in my seat, as if from my own home. Consider these Tumen Khishigten my precious support." The complex divides the warriors into three units, according to their roles at the time.

### Manlai Khishigten

The Manlai Khishigten led the column. Their task was to strike the enemy by surprise, repel it, and hold it until the main force arrived. To break up massed enemy forces, they provoked them without pause and feigned retreat. This drew the enemy toward the main force lying in ambush, so it could be surrounded and cut down from all sides. At times they attacked from the edge of enemy land or positions, or struck from behind. They wore light clothing and were armed with bows and arrows.

### Ikh Gol Khishigten

The Ikh Gol Khishigten stood in the center of the column, behind the Manlai troops. They delivered the strongest, decisive blow of the battle. For this reason, they were made up of warriors with outstanding combat skill. Their task was to weaken further and crush the enemy whose order the Manlai attack had broken. Chinggis Khaan led his army from the Ikh Gol unit. It is said that the four-legged Black Standard of war stood here.

### Tsagduul Khishigten

The Tsagduul Khishigten rode at the end of the column. They protected themselves and their horses with armor made of hardened hide. Like the Manlai and Ikh Gol units, they also had battle duties. They were to detect enemies attacking the column from behind and stop the attack. Their clothing, armor, helmets, and weapons were relatively heavy and bulky.

## How did the khishigten fight so flexibly?

The military organization of the Great Mongol State was flexible. If the enemy attacked from a side other than the front of the column, the Tsagduul troops took on the role of the Manlai, and the Manlai took on the role of the Tsagduul. In this way, the khishigten changed positions and roles to suit the situation. They were ready for an attack from any direction. ## Where does the name Songinokhairkhan come from?

There are several explanations for the name.

- **Wild onion.** Long ago, wild red onions grew thickly on the mountain's blue scree slopes. For that reason it was called "Songinot Khairkhan" (Onion Sacred Mountain), which later became Songinokhairkhan.
- **A Chinggis Khaan legend.** [Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan)](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>) was traveling on this mountain when a lion's roar was heard and the cliffs and scree began to crumble. ## What is the guardian spirit of Songinokhairkhan Mountain?

According to the learned lama Luvsanperenleijamts, Songinokhairkhan Mountain is fierce and stern. It is said to grant quickly any wish for a hard or severe undertaking. Its guardian spirit is a blue old man who rides a blue ox. He has crossed fangs and is fond of strong drink. Legend says he can sometimes take the form of a military lord. For this reason, people believed for centuries that anyone leaving for military service, war or other harsh work would be saved from harm by entrusting themselves to the old man of the mountain and praying to him. They worshipped him with offerings of arkhi (vodka) and of the first portions of their food. Families in Khuree also offered the first portions of their food and tea to Songinokhairkhan and entrusted their wishes to it.

### The fiery black fox

A legend says a fiery black fox lives on the mountain, so the winters there are snowless and the ground stays bare. People say a few have seen the small fox, with a pure white tip on its tail and a jet-black coat. The fox does not appear to everyone. Killing it is unthinkable, and people avoid even the thought of frightening it.

Khar Balgas, or Ordubalyk, lies 15 km northeast of the center of [Khotont soum](/province/arkhangai/khotont) in [Arkhangai Province (aimag)](/province/arkhangai), on the east bank of the Orkhon River. ![image](asset:tic-img-87f1da43d3844055)

## What are the key facts about Khar Balgas?

- **Location:** Arkhangai Province, 15 km northeast of the center of Khotont soum, on the east bank of the Orkhon River.
- **Founded:** 751, during the reign of Moyunchur Khan.
- **Area:** 32 km², extending 25 km to the south.

## How was Khar Balgas founded?

After the Uighurs crushed the Turkic Khaganate in 745, a man named Peilo became the Uighur khan. After his death, his son Moyunchur became khan. Under him the Uighur state reached the height of its power, and he founded the capital in the Orkhon Valley under the name Ordubalyk. Besides Ordubalyk, the Uighurs built many other towns and settlements, such as Baibalyk and Khatun. Today only the ruins of Ordubalyk and Baibalyk remain. According to written sources, the cities of Ordubalyk and Kharkhorum controlled the northern branch of the Silk Road for 400 years.

## What kind of city was Ordubalyk?

Research shows that Ordubalyk was a large city extending 25 km to the south. It had a royal palace, government offices, craft workshops, a trade guild, a religious temple, a fortified wall, horse enclosures, and military storehouses. Researchers have set the area of the city at 32 km².

- **Fortress.** The city walls have largely collapsed. Even so, the fortress is still 12 m high, and the large fortress at the center of the walls is 14 m high. Excavations inside the walls uncovered the remains of a decorated palace building.
- **Temple and steles.** In front of the walls there was a garden. Outside them stood a walled temple for the worship of Buddha images. At the temple entrance there were bright steles for the Uighur khan, with inscriptions in Uighur and Chinese.
- **Trade and crafts.** In the west and northwest of the city there were many walled buildings that seem to have been used for trade and crafts.

In the early 9th century, the Arab traveler Tamim ibn Bahr visited Ordubalyk. He described it as a populous, large city with thriving trade. Although Ordubalyk was a major city, it did not last even 100 years. Around 840 it was burned in an attack by the Yenisei Kyrgyz. According to written sources, UNESCO added Khar Balgas to the World Heritage register in 1996, and Mongolia placed it under state protection in 2002.

## What were the other Uighur cities like?

During their roughly 100 years of existence, the Uighurs are said to have founded about 10 towns and settlements. The remains of fortified towns, mostly with square clay walls, can be found today in Mongolia, Kazakhstan, and Tuva. Researchers consider these towns a "network" of fortifications. In Tuva there are the ruins of a Uighur town where, as a fortification on the northern border, an arc-shaped wall was built with many small fortresses inside.

## How did the Uighurs bury their dead?

A site called "Durvuljin" (Square), not far from Khar Balgas, was studied by a joint Mongolian-Chinese team, who determined that it is a Uighur burial.

- **Wall.** A low square clay wall, surrounded on the outside by a moat, with an entrance on the east side.
- **Building.** Inside the wall are the remains of a building made of fired brick. The walls were plastered with lime and decorated with paintings in various colors.
- **Tomb.** Underground, a dome-shaped brick tomb, like a Mongolian ger, was built, with its door facing southeast.

Uighur graves have been studied relatively little. Even so, it is known that, like the Turkic peoples, they buried horses along with the dead.

## What are the Uighur-era stone figures like?

In western Mongolia there are about 10 stone figures from the Uighur period. They stand alone, with no tomb, fence, or other structure beside them. They are relatively large figures with flat tops, wearing hats with the ear flaps turned up, and holding a cup-like object in both hands in front of the stomach. Like the Turks, the Uighurs built memorial shrines for their dead khans and nobles. One of these is the memorial shrine of Moyunchur Khan in Saikhan soum, Bulgan Province. It lay inside an earthen wall with a ditch and moat. At its center stood a bright stele with an inscription, set on a stone turtle base.

## What scripts did the Uighurs use?

The Uighurs first used the runic script. Later, drawing on the Sogdian script, they are said to have created the Old Uighur script, which suited their own language. They recorded their affairs in runic, Uighur, Sogdian, Brahmi, and Chinese scripts.

Local elders say that long ago the saxaul around the Khongor Sand Dunes grew so tall and dense that people could not find their camels inside it. The rocks of Sevrei Mountain hold many rock paintings. One shows four hunters on horseback chasing five Siberian ibex. Their bows have a simple shape, and researchers believe they may be Bronze Age bows.

## Why is the Singing Dune called "singing"?

The highest part of the Khongor Sand Dunes is called the Singing Dune. It is taller than a nine-story building. From its top, you can clearly hear people, animals and the wind in the distance. Looking down, vehicles at the base look like tiny insects. If you lie on the sand and look up, you hear a sound like an airplane humming. You wait for the plane to appear, but none comes. In fact, this is the sound of sand warmed by the sun, sliding gently in the wind. That is why locals named it the "Singing Dune."

## How do I travel around the Khongor Sand Dunes?

Several tourist ger camps stand behind the dunes. From there, you can ride horses or camels to reach the sand. When a strong wind raises a sandstorm, you cannot see anything around you. For this reason, the camps are located 5–17 km from the dunes. When you walk on the dunes, start from the nearest hill and climb at an angle. It is easiest to follow the ridge from one peak to the next. The sand is loose and shifts, so you cannot climb straight up. Your feet sink in, and you tire quickly.

## What is the legend of the Khongor Sand Dunes?

Locals have long worshipped Khatan Sevrei Mountain, which rises south of the dunes, as a protector of women. This is why it carries the title "khatan" (queen). A legend links four places: Tol Khad in Noyon soum, which borders Sevrei soum, Noyon Bogd Mountain, Khatan Sevrei Mountain and the Khongor Sand Dunes. Long ago, a lord decided to send away his wife, Sevrei, who was known for her bad temper. His official advised him to do it, to teach her a lesson. Angry and upset, Queen Sevrei threw her mirror on the road as soldiers escorted her. The mirror broke into many pieces, and one piece formed Tol Khad (Mirror Rock), it is said. The lord scolded her for her temper, but he missed the wife he had lived with for many years. He would come to Tol Khad to see her face. That, they say, is how Noyon Mountain came to stand in front of it. In exile, Queen Sevrei sank into sadness every day. She sat on the riverbank, combing her jet-black braids and lost in thought. Among the soldiers guarding her was a hero named Khongor. He watched the queen with pity and fell in love with her. Khongor begged her to forget everything and start a new life with him in this land. However much he pleaded, Queen Sevrei ignored him and sang ever more sorrowful songs. Many years passed, and Tol Khad grew dim and gray. Khongor, at his wit's end, wanted to make the path to his beloved's river softer. He gathered every stone on the way under his body, it is said, and turned into sand dunes. Legend says this is how the Khongor Sand Dunes were formed.

They are about 180 km long and cover 965 km². The widest part is 27 km.

@@ FIELD seoTitle text
Ikhesiin Gazar: Where Mongol Khans Were Honored
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Mongol khans' tombs: why have they never been found? Ikhesiin Gazar, a worship site set apart from secret burials, from the Xiongnu to the Mongol Empire.
@@ FIELD body richText
Ikhesiin Gazar is a concept found in The Secret History of the Mongols. It does not mean a burial place for khans or nobles. It means a place far from the graves where people paid respect to them and held worship ceremonies. To this day, not a single tomb of the Golden Family khans has been found in Mongolia. Mongols kept the burial places of their khans strictly secret. Many scholars are still trying to solve the mystery.

## What is Ikhesiin Gazar? Quick facts

- **Meaning:** a place with statues where people paid respect from afar to khans and high nobles and held worship ceremonies.
- **Source:** The Secret History of the Mongols mentions "ikhesiin gazar" and worship ceremonies.
- **Related monuments:** sites with stone figures from the 13th–14th centuries.
- **Distinctive feature:** it is separate from the burial place and located far away.

## How were ancient khans buried?

### The Xiongnu period

Until the time of the [Xiongnu (Hunnu)](</about/history/khunnu>) state, tombs of khans and nobles were built with large, impressive stone structures. This was a way of showing them respect. Tombs of nobles often hold many valuable objects, but children's burials rarely contain any. Wives, queens, and servants were buried together with the ruler. This was a tragic custom of the Xiongnu, Turkic, and Uighur periods. One Xiongnu noble was buried with 28 people, from 7 months to 45 years old. Genetic studies have shown that they were not from one family. The tombs of queens and subjects also held plenty of goods. Later, however, this became the main reason people and rivals of the time looted the tombs.

### The Xianbei period

From the Xianbei period, burials of high nobles were covered with earth and hidden in secret. The earlier tradition of holding burial and worship ceremonies together changed. Burial structures were made hidden, and worship ceremonies gradually moved to open places.

### The Turkic and Uighur periods

From the Turkic and Uighur periods, burial and worship ceremonies for khans and nobles were held separately, and this made the tombs of khans secret. A burial chamber was built deep underground and covered with earth, without using stones. Later it looked like an ordinary mound of earth. People avoided naming these places directly and called them "Shoroon Bumbagar," "Undurkhangai," and "Khangai." Many records show that large worship structures with stone figures were built about 120–150 km southeast of the burial site. Offerings were made there every year.

## How were the great Mongol khans buried in secret?

There are many theories and studies about the secret burials of the great Mongol khans, but no shared conclusion has been reached. Some historical sources mention that the ground was "trampled by a thousand horses." After several centuries, ground trampled by many horses looks no different from ordinary barren steppe. The Mongol nobility paid respect not at the burial place of khans and nobles, but at worship sites far from it. As people made offerings from afar, the burial places became even more hidden. The Setsen Khan, Tushet Khan, Zasagt Khan, and Sain Noyon Khan were all khans of the direct Golden Family line, so they kept the ancient customs. Only men of the Golden Family took part in their burials. Sons-in-law and daughters-in-law were not allowed. This is seen as proof that the secrecy was strictly kept.

## Where was Chinggis Khaan buried?

There is no record of exactly where Ogedei Khan and the khans after him were buried. No tombs of Mongol khans have been found in the lands that were part of the Mongol Empire either. When [Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan)](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>) died while fighting in Tangut, his cart broke down, but the sources clearly state that his body was brought back and buried in his homeland. It is believed this tradition was followed for all the khans. Archaeologist D. Erdenebaatar has explained that Ordos is not the tomb of the great khan. It is a place of worship ceremonies where people honored khans and nobles, and there is no tomb there.

## What is a worship ceremony?

The Secret History of the Mongols clearly says "we will go to the ikhesiin gazar." This is not a place where a khan was buried. It is a place with statues where people paid respect from afar. Sites in Mongolia with stone figures from the 13th–14th centuries are considered "ikhesiin gazar." There, people shared the food of the worship ceremony and honored the memory of their khans and lords. A worship ceremony means making offerings with livestock. People offered sheep and horses, presented the food to the fire, and brought out the best parts, believing this pleased the owner. Later, Buddhism spread deeply in Mongolia, and its teaching against taking life spread. The custom of offering livestock in worship ceremonies then ended.

## Frequently asked questions

It is not a burial place for khans or nobles. It is a place with stone figures where people paid respect from afar and held worship ceremonies.

### Why have the tombs of Mongol khans not been found?

The tombs were kept strictly secret. Khans were buried deep underground and covered with earth, worship sites were built far from the burial, and only men of the Golden Family took part in burials.

### When did Mongols stop offering livestock in worship ceremonies?

The custom ended when Buddhism spread deeply and its teaching against taking life became widespread.

## What halls are there in the museum?

### Hall of the historical development of parliament

In the ancient states, the laws and decrees issued by the khans were binding across the country. From the 3rd century BC to the early 20th century, the power to make laws belonged to the ruler. The first Constitution of the Mongolian People's Republic in 1924 set up a two-tier parliament: the State Great Khural and the State Little Khural. This system changed over time and became today's State Great Khural.

### Hall of the historical development of the judiciary

In the ancient states, judicial power rested directly with the khan and the officials he appointed. In the early and middle 20th century, the judiciary worked largely as an extension of the legislature. The 1992 Constitution made the judiciary independent of the other branches. This allowed courts to answer only to the law.

### Hall of the historical tradition of statehood

Over more than 2,200 years of Mongolian statehood, dozens of empires were founded on Mongolian land. More than 200 khans and rulers governed them. A head of state is a person with defined authority who is recognized across the whole country, such as a khan or a president.

### Hall of the historical development of the executive branch

In the ancient Mongolian states, officials served under the khan's unlimited power. This is seen as an early form of executive government. In 1911, by decree of the Bogd Jibzundamba Khan, the first government with five ministries was founded. This was the first executive branch. A more specialized structure followed, and the idea of a Prime Minister took hold. At the time the source was written, 29 Prime Ministers had served in Mongolian history.

Burkhan Khaldun is a sacred mountain deeply rooted in the hearts and faith of the Mongols. People have worshiped it since the time of Bodonchar Munkhag, ancestor of the Khiad Borjigin. In the time of [Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan)](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>), it was worshiped as the sacred mountain of the Khamag Mongol and then of the Great Mongol State. Today it is a mountain of state worship and lies within the [Khan Khentii](</destination/protected/khan-khentii>) Strictly Protected Area.

## What are the key facts about Burkhan Khaldun?

- **Location:** [Khentii Province (aimag)](/province/khentii), Khan Khentii Strictly Protected Area.
- **The Secret History of the Mongols:** mentioned 27 times under its main name.
- **Official worship:** since 1818.
- **Strictly protected area:** in 1992, an area of 12,000 km² was established as the Khan Khentii protected area.
- **Mountain of state worship:** presidential decrees of 1995, 2004 and 2009.
- **UNESCO:** inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Which mountain is the real Burkhan Khaldun?

In early times, the whole Khan Khentii range was called Burkhan Khaldun. The Khentii Range has 13 places named Burkhan Khaldun, and the highest peak among them kept the name. Bazargur, a researcher at the Institute of Geography of the Academy of Sciences, offered a different idea. He argues that when the Taichuud attacked Temujin as he lived on the bank of the Burgi, Temujin fled in a single day to Munkhjargalan Mountain, 24 km from the Burgi bank, and lived there for many years. So, he says, the true mountain of state worship, Burkhan Khaldun, should be Munkhjargalan Mountain. It is also said that Chinggis Khaan worshiped Munkhjargalan before going to war.

## What is the nature of Burkhan Khaldun like?

The back and front slopes of the mountain are covered with dense forest. Near the summit, the trees thin out and only stunted taiga plants grow. The summit has a wide, open area. In its center is a platform that looks like an overturned trough, as if made by human hands. If you go down from the main ovoo (sacred stone cairn) at the summit, you will find a clear lake in an oval gorge on the slope.

## What ovoos are there on Burkhan Khaldun?

The mountain has three ovoos: upper, middle and lower.

- **Lower ovoo** — at the foot of the mountain.
- **Middle ovoo** — on the middle part of the mountain. It takes about two hours on a good horse from the small ovoo at the foot. There is a bronze cauldron about one meter in diameter here.
- **Upper ovoo** — on the platform at the summit, steep and taller than a ten-story building.

## How has Burkhan Khaldun been worshiped?

Official worship of Burkhan Khaldun began in 1818. In 1992, the State Little Khural and a government resolution reaffirmed the mountain's protected status, and the Khan Khentii Strictly Protected Area was established. In 1995 President P. Ochirbat, in 2004 President N. Bagabandi and in 2009 President Ts. Elbegdorj issued decrees designating Burkhan Khaldun a "Mountain of State Worship." The state ceremony to worship the mountain's sky was set to take place once every four years. According to sources, state worship ceremonies were held in 1998, 2002, 2006, 2010 and 2015.

## Why is Burkhan Khaldun a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Burkhan Khaldun was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site on the following grounds:

- It is mentioned 27 times under its main name in The Secret History of the Mongols.
- It has preserved the customs of the Mongol people in full.
- It is an untouched sacred mountain (khairkhan) where no archaeological traces have been found and no research has been done.

## What is part of the Khan Khentii Strictly Protected Area?

The Khan Khentii Strictly Protected Area, which includes Burkhan Khaldun Khairkhan (sacred mountain), covers the river basins of the Onon, Kherlen and [Tuul](</destination/heritage/place/tuul-gol>) rivers. It protects many burial sites, khirigsuur (burial mounds), stone figures, deer stones, the ruins of Sarid Monastery, Khagiin Khar Lake and the [Uglugchiin Kherem](</destination/heritage/place/uglugchiin-kherem>) fortress wall. These historical and cultural sites and scenic natural places are protected together.

## What is Tsegtseriin Shar Steppe? Quick facts

- **The Secret History of the Mongols:** sections 61, 67, 94.
- **Location:** according to scholar O. Sukhbaatar, near the western edge of the Gal River valley.
- **Related people:** Yesugei the Brave, Temujin, Dai Sechen of the Khongirad, Lady Borte.
- **Main events:** the betrothal, the poisoning of Yesugei the Brave, and the bringing home of Borte.

## How was Temujin betrothed to Borte?

When Temujin was nine, his father Yesugei the Brave took his son to the Olkhunuud people, the family of Mother Hoelun, to ask for a bride. On the way, between Tsegtser and Chikhurgu, they met Dai Sechen of the Khongirad. Dai Sechen asked Yesugei where he was going. Yesugei said he was taking his son to ask for a bride from his maternal relatives. Looking at Temujin, Dai Sechen said, "He is a boy with fire in his eyes and light on his face." He told of a dream from the night before. A white gyrfalcon had flown in, holding the sun and the moon in its claws, and landed on his hand. He said this was a sign that Yesugei would bring his son. He said the Khongirad had long been famous for the beauty of their daughters. Their sons guarded the homeland, and their daughters were chosen for their looks. Dai Sechen invited Yesugei to his home and showed him his daughter. She had light on her face and fire in her eyes. Her name was Borte, and she pleased Yesugei. Dai Sechen said, "A girl's fate is not to grow old in the doorway where she was born." He agreed to give his daughter and asked that the boy stay as his son-in-law. Yesugei the Brave agreed. He warned, "My son is easily frightened by dogs. My in-law, please do not let dogs frighten my son." He gave his spare horse as a gift and left Temujin as the future son-in-law.

## How was Yesugei the Brave poisoned?

On the way back, Yesugei the Brave met Tatar people holding a wedding feast on the Tsegtseriin Shar steppe. He was thirsty, so he stopped at the feast. The Tatars recognized him. They remembered that he had once raided them and wanted revenge, so they secretly put poison in his food. Yesugei the Brave felt unwell on the road, and he grew worse after reaching home. When Temujin arrived at the ger, his father had already died. It is said that Temujin wept bitterly for his father.

## How did Temujin bring Borte home?

Temujin stayed at Dai Sechen's home for about ten days and then left. For twelve years he did not see Borte, but he kept thinking of her. He and Boorchu followed the thieves eastward to recover eight stolen light bay horses. They came close to Dai Sechen's land, but had no chance to search for her. He took the eight horses to [Khukh Lake at Khar Zurkh](</destination/landscapes/khar-zurkhnii-khukh-nuur>). This calmed Mother Hoelun and his younger brother Khasar, who had been worried about him. Later he took his younger brother Belgutei and went downstream along the Kherlen River to look for Lady Borte, whom his father had arranged for him. They found Dai Sechen living between Tsegtser and Chikhurgu. Dai Sechen was overjoyed to see Temujin. He said, "I heard that the Tayichuud, your kinsmen, were jealous of you, and I was worried and in despair. At last we meet." He sent Lady Borte with him. His wife Chotan sent their daughter off with a valuable black sable coat as her dowry. Dai Sechen himself escorted them as far as the meadow of Urag Desert on the Kherlen before turning back.

## Why was Dai Sechen so loyal?

After Yesugei was poisoned, the strength of Khamag Mongol weakened. The Tatars roamed freely across Khongirad land. For more than ten years, especially the last seven or eight, it was clearly very hard for Dai Sechen to keep the beautiful Lady Borte hidden from public view. Yet he stayed true to his word to give his daughter to Yesugei. He united the couple and escorted them to the border of his land. Then he turned back, honoring the customs of Ambagai Khan.

## What is there to know about Nemegt Mountain at a glance?

- **Location:** Umnugovi Province, 60 km north of Gurvantes soum and 320 km from the provincial center.

## What does Nemegt Mountain look like?

## What plants and animals live around Nemegt Mountain?

The vegetation of Nemegt Mountain is distinctive. Plants on the back side of the mountain are relatively large and grow well. ## What dinosaurs have been found at Nemegt?

Nemegt Mountain, Altan Uul, Tugrugiin Shiree, Ukhaa Tolgod, and Khermen Tsav are known worldwide for their dinosaur fossils. International expeditions regularly do research there.

### Nemegtosaurus

In 1946, a joint Russian-Mongolian expedition led by the well-known Russian writer and scientist I. A. Efremov found a huge four-legged, plant-eating dinosaur in the Great Nemegt Canyon. They named it Nemegtosaurus (Nemegtosaurus) after the mountain. It belongs to the sauropods, which ate plants. Since then, many Mongolian, Russian, and Polish expeditions have worked here. They have uncovered many fossils of ancient animals, plants, and dinosaurs, which are kept in natural history museums in Russia, Europe, and Mongolia.

### Oviraptor

Since 1993, an expedition led by M. Novacek of the American Museum of Natural History has done research here. About 30 km east of Nemegt, at Ukhaa Tolgod on the border of Gurvantes and [Sevrei](</province/umnugov/sevrei>) soums, they found many interesting fossils. One of them is an Oviraptor sitting on its nest of eggs. Oviraptor is considered an ancestor of today's birds. In 1923, the Roy Chapman Andrews expedition found the first Oviraptor fossil at Bayanzag, next to a Protoceratops nest, and named it the "egg thief." The Ukhaa Tolgod find showed that it was actually brooding and protecting its eggs. Even so, the name "egg thief," used in paleontology for 70 years, has stayed.

### Tarbosaurus bataar

In 2012, a Tarbosaurus bataar skeleton, illegally taken out of the Mongolian Gobi, was about to be sold at the Heritage Auction house in New York, USA. The planned sale caused a scandal. Research proved that the fossil came from the Nemegt Valley. The auction was canceled and the fossil was returned to Mongolia. It was studied by Philip Currie, a paleontologist and professor at the University of Alberta in Canada (Mongolians call him Phil, and he has done research at Nemegt many times), his wife Eva Koppelhus, M. Norell, a paleontologist at the American Museum of Natural History, and M. Bolortsetseg, a Mongolian paleontologist. They determined that it came from the Nemegt Valley in Umnugovi. The well-known Mongolian paleontologist Dr. R. Barsbold has shown that the name "bataar" comes from the Mongolian word "baatar" (hero). To learn more about Mongolian dinosaur fossils, read the page about the [Natural History Museum](</things-to-do/museums/baigaliin-tuukhiin-muzei>).

## What is the nature like on Bogdkhan Mountain?

Spruce forest is the main plant community of the taiga zone. Mongolia has only three such forests, and one of them is on Bogdkhan Mountain. In recent years, however, it has shrunk considerably. Bogdkhan Mountain is the southernmost limit for many mammal species of the great Siberian taiga and the Khentii Range taiga. Small rivers and streams rise from about 20 valleys around the mountain. Some flow into the [Tuul River](</destination/heritage/place/tuul-gol>), while others reach hollows at the foot of the mountain and soak into the ground. They help create a favorable climate for the city.

## When did Bogdkhan Mountain become protected?

The valleys and slopes of Bogdkhan Mountain hold historical monuments, including rocks with inscriptions and paintings, khirigsuur (burial mounds), statues and graves.

- **13th century.** Legend says Toghrul Wang Khan protected and worshipped the mountain and named it Khan Mountain.
- **1294.** Chapter 398 of the law code of the Great Yuan state is said to have placed scenic areas under protection. These included Bogd Khan Mountain, Otgontenger, Khan Khentii and the great Altai passes.
- **September 24, 1778.** At the request of Khuree minister Yundendorj, the Qing emperor issued a decree making the mountain a strictly protected area.
- **1809.** The mountain's valleys were put under guard. Entry permits had to be issued by the Bogd Khan himself.
- **1957, 1974 and 1995.** Its protected status was reconfirmed.

## How was Bogdkhan Mountain worshipped?

From 1778, the mountain was worshipped twice a year, in spring and autumn. On the main peak, Tsetsee Gun, there are two ovoo (sacred stone cairns), known as the religious ovoo and the state ovoo.

- **Offerings.** Each ovoo received food, a whole cow's meat and the meat of several sheep. The offering platform was as large as the roof ring of a ger with ten walls. Provinces, banners (khoshuu) and ordinary people sent dairy products, airag (fermented mare's milk) and yogurt. These were gathered at Chuluut valley a month ahead. On the day of the ceremony, camels carried them up to Tsetsee Gun.
- **Ceremony.** Before sunrise on the ceremony day, state nobles rode up through [Turkhurakh valley](</destination/heritage/place/turkhurakhyn-am>). More than 50 lamas came from the Great Khuree, led by the tsogchin unzad and gesgui. About 40 lamas came from [Manzushir Monastery](</destination/heritage/place/manzushiryn-khiid>) to read scriptures.
- **Sharing.** After the ceremony, the offered food was shared among 30 provinces and 10 datsans (monastic colleges).
- **Title.** The two peaks, Tsetsee and Tushee, were granted the title "gun" (duke). Each year, 50 lan of silver was paid as their salary. During the ceremony it was placed in a hollow at the summit, wrapped in khadag (ceremonial silk scarves).

Later, an octagonal temple with a golden finial was built on the summit for the worship ceremony. Two lamas lived there permanently, lighting lamps and burning incense. Guards of the Bogd mountain patrolled in weekly shifts and delivered lamp oil and food.

## What is the legend of the Khangarid guardian?

The guardian spirit of Bogdkhan Mountain is honored as the mighty Khangarid bird and the White Old Man riding a deer. In the tsam (masked dance) of the Great Khuree, both figures took part. The Khangarid bird defeated all evil, and the White Old Man brought sacred peace. According to the [Khangarid](</destination/heritage/place/khangarid-sakhius>) legend, in ancient times the daughters of the water spirits were sleeping at dawn. The fifth daughter woke up in terror from a nightmare. A black, spotted snake was about to bite her. A khangarid flew in, seized the snake by the neck and tail, and clamped it in its beak. It said, "I will guard the entrance of your jade-white palace," and flew away with the snake. It is said that this is how the city of Khuree got its Khangarid guardian.
