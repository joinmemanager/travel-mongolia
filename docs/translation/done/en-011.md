Aidasyn Pass is a mountain pass with an ovoo (sacred stone cairn) and a worship ceremony. It lies in the gorge before the airport, on the road from [Ulaanbaatar](/province/ulaanbaatar) into Tuv Province (aimag). In the past, anyone traveling to Beijing, or from Beijing to Khuree, had to cross this main pass.

## Aidasyn Pass: quick facts

- **Location:** On the road from Ulaanbaatar into Tuv Province, in the gorge before the airport.
- **Historical role:** The main pass on the Beijing–Khuree road.
- **Ovoo:** Yes, with worship ceremonies.
- **Name explanation:** A legend about "fear," and a linguistic explanation, "Baits Pass."
- **Nearby places:** Songsolon Hill, Songsolon Crossing.

## Who used to travel over Aidasyn Pass?

Travelers and caravan drivers crossed this pass. Officials of high state rank were also greeted and received here. On arriving and departing, the officials would stop at the pass and brush off the dust. They would walk around the ovoo and whisper their wishes to it.

## How did Aidasyn Pass get its name?

### The legend of "fear and chills"

Officials of the Manchu Qing state came with many attendants. They were arrogant and quick to anger. The lower the rank of the Khuree official who greeted them, the more they put on airs. The Khuree officials knew this. They pretended to be afraid and showed great respect. Among themselves, though, they mocked the visitors: "Here come those fearful chills again." It is said that this is how the pass came to be called Aidasyn Pass.

### The "Baits Pass" explanation

O. Sukhbaatar, a researcher of place names, explained the name differently from a linguistic point of view. According to his research, the name has nothing to do with fear:

- It was first called **Baits Pass**.
- Over time it became **Baidas Pass**.
- Later the "B" dropped off, and it came to be called **Aidasyn Pass**.

Names of mountains and waters are usually given for their shape or for historical events. Still, language change often carries them far from their original names.

## What is Songsolon Hill?

Urgent news was passed along by mounted relay messengers, station by station. From Aidasyn Pass, a messenger would report how many officials were coming, how many attendants they had, and what mounts and carts they used. The messenger on Songsolon Hill then passed this on to the relay post at Songsolon Crossing. For this reason, the place where the bridge crosses the [Tuul River](</destination/heritage/place/tuul-gol>) came to be called Songsolon Crossing. The hill next to it came to be called Songsolon Hill. With this quick news, people could carefully carry out the customs of welcoming honored guests and hosting them at a feast, it is said.

Most stone figures erected by the Turks have survived without heads. It is believed that the Uighurs, who came to Mongolian lands after the Turks, destroyed many of their monuments. The National Museum of Mongolian History holds the headless statues of the Turkic Bilge Khan and his queen.

## What do medieval stone figures look like?

Turkic-era items found in the memorial complexes of Bilge Khan and the commander Kul Tegin, including gold and silver objects, stone figures and inscribed stelae.

### When were the Khushuu Tsaidam monuments discovered?

N. M. Yadrintsev discovered them in 1889.
