## What does the ger symbolize?

The ger is considered the hearth of Mongolia, a symbol of peace and harmony, and an expression of independence. It also represents the aspirations of the Mongol people and those of Mongol descent. It reflects the norms of Mongolian customs, traditions, culture and art. Mongolian craftsmen built the ger with great skill. Turtogtokh led this huge task. The builders aimed to depict the high heaven and the layers of the cloudy world around the ger. It is decorated with steel carving that uses 1,380 kinds of gemstones, elephant ivory and other materials, including six large turquoise pieces. On the red sandalwood lock bar, the words "State Ceremonial Ger" are made in silver in the Uighur-Mongol script.
- **Silk embroidered door.** This door honors visitors with the words "Peaceful State, Ten Thousand Peace". Its outer side is sky blue. After you open the three doors with proper ceremony, the inside of the great eight-wall ger opens before your eyes. It has no support posts and is set under the Eternal Blue Sky.

Rashaan Rock: Rock Art in Batshireet, Khentii

Rashaan Rock is in Batshireet soum, [Khentii Province (aimag)](/province/khentii), on a rocky outcrop at the southeast foot of Binder Mountain. Archaeologists describe it as a rich complex of historical and cultural monuments from the Stone Age to the 16th century in eastern Mongolia.

![image](asset:tic-img-d764b8cd1f00dde5)

## What is Rashaan Rock? Quick facts

- **Location:** Khentii Province, Batshireet soum, southeast foot of Binder Mountain.
- **Period:** from the Stone Age to the 16th century.
- **Monuments:** rock paintings, hundreds of clan tamgas (marks), over 20 inscriptions, stone tools, burials.
- **Inscription languages:** Khitan, Arabic, Persian, Tibetan, Chinese, Mongolian, Turkic.
- **Researcher:** Kh. Perlee, who studied the site from 1943.

## What is there to see at Rashaan Rock?

The area has Stone Age rock paintings, various inscriptions, stone tools, bronze and iron tools, and many burials from the [Xiongnu (Hunnu)](</about/history/khunnu>) period. Historical monuments from the Bronze Age, the Xiongnu, the Khitan and the Mongol Empire are also preserved here. Animals and human figures were pecked into the rock, and hundreds of clan tamgas were carved. There are also over 20 inscriptions in Khitan, Arabic, Persian, Tibetan, Chinese and Mongolian scripts.

## What rock art can you see at Rashaan Rock?

Kh. Perlee studied the site in 1943 and 1968. He recorded 180 figures and signs on the east-facing front of the granite, more than 40 on the back and more than 50 on the ridge.

- **Mammoth.** On a rock on the west side, the body outline of a mammoth was pecked into the stone. It has a high back, short thick legs and a long trunk. It closely resembles mammoth images in the Kapova Cave in the Urals and in caves in Europe.
- **Woolly rhinoceros.** On a flat rock, three woolly rhinoceroses walk together. They are shown with huge bodies, high humps, short legs, curved horns on their noses and tusks.

## What do the Turkic inscriptions at Rashaan Rock say?

The scholar Kh. Perlee discovered and studied the tamgas and writing of ancient nomads on the rock in 1961. In 1979, a joint Mongolian-Soviet historical and cultural expedition studied the inscriptions in detail and found a second Turkic inscription.

- **First inscription.** S. Kharjubai and Kh. Perlee read it as "atam apam achignun" and translated it as "the reward and blessing of our forefathers."
- **Second inscription.** The line is 60 cm long, and the letters are 17–20 cm high. North of the inscription is an image of an ibex. It was read as "sat atych yerti" and translated as "He was a brave grandson."

## Why is the Stone Age settlement at Rashaan Rock important?

Kh. Perlee began the research in 1943. He visited almost every year from 1960 to 1980 and published a special study of the many tamgas on the rock. Stone Age research began in 1973 and 1974 with a test excavation by the Gurvan Gol survey team. In 1980–1981, a joint Mongolian-Soviet expedition excavated the site and found four cultural layers at a depth of 2.5–3.5 m. The excavation produced about 2,500 stone tools and bones of several animal species. A woolly rhinoceros tusk found at a depth of 1.5 m was an especially important find. Early Paleolithic people around the Khentii Range, including Rashaan Rock, made tools from various kinds of flint. They lived by hunting animals such as the Mongolian wild ass (khulan), mammoth and woolly rhinoceros. They left images of these animals on the rock faces.

## How did ancient people's lives change?

Between 15,000 and 8,000 years ago, the climate of Central Asia changed greatly, and large animals began to die out. People had to hunt smaller, faster animals. They began making precise long-range tools such as bows and arrows, using the springiness of wood. Their lives became more nomadic. Stone tools of this period found in Mongolia, especially those that scholars call the "Gobi remnants" type, have matching finds in southern Siberia, the Russian Far East, northern China, Korea, Hokkaido in Japan, and Alaska in America.

There are over 20 inscriptions in Khitan, Arabic, Persian, Tibetan, Chinese and Mongolian scripts, plus two Turkic inscriptions.

@@ FIELD seoTitle text
Morin Tolgoi deer stones: Altanbulag soum, Tuv
@@ FIELD seoDescription textarea
Morin Tolgoi deer stones: three stones found among 120 square tombs and 24 Xiongnu tombs on the Tuul River's north bank, with twisted deer rumps.
@@ FIELD region text

@@ FIELD description richText
The Morin Tolgoi deer stones are in Altanbulag soum, [Tuv Province (aimag)](/province/tuv), at the foot of Morin Tolgoi Mountain on the north bank of the [Tuul River](</destination/heritage/place/tuul-gol>). The site has 120 square tombs and 24 Xiongnu (Hunnu) tombs. In one square tomb in the southeast, three deer stones form the west, north, and east walls.

## What is there to know about Morin Tolgoi?

- **Location:** Altanbulag soum, Tuv Province, north bank of the Tuul River, at the foot of Morin Tolgoi Mountain.
- **Tombs:** 120 square tombs and 24 Xiongnu tombs.
- **Deer stones:** three, used in the walls of one square tomb.
- **Date:** late Bronze Age, 12th–8th century BC.
- **Special feature:** deer with their rumps carved in a twisted pose.

## How were the stones found?

Researchers first examined and recorded the deer stones in the west and east walls of the tomb. The stone in the north wall was sunk into the ground, so it was not recognized at first. It was later excavated and studied.

## What does deer stone no. 1 look like?

This stone formed the west wall of the tomb. It is made of fine-grained, green-gray granite. An elongated stone was carved directly, without shaping it first. A thick layer of red lichen covers the surface, so the carvings look pale.

- **Front.** A sun at the top, and below it three deer, large and small, with their muzzles pointing down.
- **West side.** One deer with its muzzle pointing up, and below it a five-sided shield with a palate pattern.
- **Back.** Three deer, large and small, a dagger, a round mirror, a leopard, and an animal of the elk family, all shown in hollowed-out pecked carving.
- **East side.** It is also carved.
- **Size:** 2 m high, 42 cm wide, and 23 cm thick.

All the deer on the stone are elegantly stylized. They have long, narrow, bird-like beaks and antlers with many branches that curl like scrolls. Their bodies are slim, with rounded hips, long narrow ears, and large round eyes, as if leaping. One deer is carved at the far left of the back. Its rump did not fit on that surface, so the carver twisted it around to the other side. This is interesting.

## What do deer stones no. 2 and no. 3 look like?

### Deer stone no. 2

This stone was used in the east wall of the tomb. Its top has recently broken off, and its lower part is missing. On the sun-facing side, there is a sun at the top and a small deer with its head pointing down. On the wide side, two deer, one large and one small, are shown at the top with their muzzles pointing up. The rump of the large deer was cut off when the stone broke. To the left of the two deer, a small ibex is shown inside a circle. The deer on this stone are also stylized.

### Deer stone no. 3

This stone formed the north wall of the tomb. On the sun-facing side, there is a sun at the top. Four deer, large and small, with their muzzles pointing up, spiral from the lower left to the upper right of the stone. Between the deer on the front, a bow and arrow are carved. On the lower part of the left side, there are a mirror, a battle hammer, and a dagger with a round pommel. The dagger is 44 cm long.

## Why are the Morin Tolgoi stones important to science?

The dagger, battle hammer, and mirror on the stones date to the late Bronze Age, the 12th–8th century BC. The technique of twisting the bodies of deer and other animals was widespread among tribes living in the Altai in the 5th–4th century BC. It is known as the "Altai feature" of animal-style art. The Bronze Age deer stones of Morin Tolgoi show deer with twisted rumps, and they are much older than those from the Altai. This makes them very interesting for researchers. V.V. Volkov published a picture of a deer stone with this kind of carving, but he did not say clearly where the stone came from. In 1983, researchers found a Bronze Age drawing of a deer with a twisted rump on the rocks of Uvgunt in [Buregkhangai soum](/province/bulgan/buregkhangai), [Bulgan Province](/province/bulgan). These facts suggest that this unusual technique began in the Bronze Age, when artists twisted animals in rock paintings and deer stones to fit them into the available space. Later, people in the Altai developed the technique to fit the shapes of their wood and bone objects. Many finds from early Iron Age tombs in the Central and Mountain Altai confirm this.

### How many tombs are there at Morin Tolgoi?

There are 120 square tombs and 24 Xiongnu tombs.

The twisted deer rumps show that this technique is many centuries older than the Altai "animal-style art."

The ruins of Ongiin Monastery lie in [Dundgovi Province (aimag)](/province/dundgov), in [Saikhan-Ovoo soum](</province/dundgov/saikhan-ovoo>). They stand on the slope of Saikhan-Ovoo Mountain, on the east bank of a scenic, green bend of the Ongi River. In Tibetan, the monastery was called "Gunjambaalin," or "Giver of Merit." ## What are the key facts about Ongiin Monastery?

- **Location:** Dundgovi Province, Saikhan-Ovoo soum, east bank of the Ongi River.
- **Tibetan name:** Gunjambaalin ("Giver of Merit").
- **Built:** 1760–1810, by Khutagt lama Ishdonkhundev and his student Bari lama Damtsagdorj.
- **At its peak:** 28 temples, more than 1,000 lamas and four datsans (monastic colleges).
- **Ger museum:** Founded in 2001.

## What are the Three Monasteries of Ongi?

Another monastery, called "Khoshuu Khurliin Monastery," once stood 15 km from Ongiin Monastery, on the bank of the Ongi River. Together with the Khutagt Lama's Monastery on the west flank and Khamba Damtsagdorj's Monastery on the east flank, it was known as the "Three Monasteries of Ongi." The monastery had four teaching datsans. They taught philosophy, tantric meditation, medicine (mamba) and astrology and mathematics. The last abbot of the monastery, Luvsandorj, was arrested in 1937.

## What was the layout of Ongiin Monastery?

Builders carefully cut terraces into the rocky mountain slope and leveled them. On the tiered high platforms, they raised distinct and elaborate buildings. This clearly took a great deal of labor and time. Today everything lies in ruins, and the site feels like what is left of a former town.

- **Khutagt Lama's Monastery.** It stood west of the main assembly temple. It had a large blue brick enclosure with Lantsa script patterns. Inside were nine temples with glazed brick roofs. To the west and east were stupas with prayer wheels. Outside the enclosure were two large temples, and a large white stupa stood to the west.
- **Khamba Damtsagdorj's Monastery.** It stood east of the main assembly temple. A source says it had a history of 220 years. It had 13 elegant temples built up the mountainside. On a flat stone at the monastery's upper ovoo (sacred stone cairn), an image of [White Umbrella Buddha](</about/culture/religion/tsagaan-shukhert>) is carved.

A wooden bridge spanned the Ongi River. During floods, water ran over the bridge. Today, tourist ger camps such as Tsagaan-Ovoo and Ongiin Nuuts stand near the monastery.

## What is there to see at the Ongiin Monastery ruins today?

- **Five ovoos.** Five ovoos used for worship ceremonies remain on the surrounding mountains: Durvuljin Shashir, Burkhant Shashir, Luvsan Jamba, Bumbat and Saikhan-Ovoo.
- **Stomach spring.** The mineral spring at the east edge of the ruins is protected by a shaded enclosure. It has been turned into a well that is drawn with a ladle.
- **Stairs of Merit.** In the southern part of the ruins is a staircase where a conch shell was blown to call lamas to assembly.
- **Eastern temple (Zuun Gudeng).** This rebuilt temple stands in the middle of the ruins. In summer, young lamas stay here and read scriptures for visitors. The temple holds images and statues of [Buddha Shakyamuni](</about/culture/religion/burkhan-bagsh>), the ten protector deities, a portrait of the temple's founder Bari lama Damtsagdorj, and the Three Deities of Longevity. It also holds images and statues of [Goviin Lkha](/about/culture/religion/goviin-lkha-burkhan), [Maidar](/about/culture/religion/maidar-burkhan), [Bogd Zonkhov](/about/culture/religion/bogd-zonkhov) and [Lovon Badamjunai](/about/culture/religion/lovon-badamjunai).

### Ger museum

The ger museum was founded in 2001. It holds the 21-volume "Sumbum" scripture written by Bari lama Damtsagdorj. It also displays his skull cup and his trumpet made from a human thighbone. Other items include copper drums used by the monastery's lamas, vessels and dishes, a garment called lagainamjir, a wooden trough for kneading ritual dough, a stone mortar for grinding tea, bronze cauldrons, tripods, a drill for boring wood, a Mongolian scale, and a camel-loaded trunk. You can also see building decorations that survived when the monastery was destroyed.

### Luvaanjalbuu statue

On the rocky mountain southwest of the monastery stands a seated statue of Luvaanjalbuu, the king of the nagas (water spirits). It is 2 m tall and weighs 1.3 tons. Local people had it made in the hope that the Ongi River would flow again.

Noen Uul Tombs: Xiongnu Elite Burial Site

The Noen Uul Tombs are a burial site of [Xiongnu (Hunnu)](</about/history/khunnu>) nobles in [Noen Uul](</destination/protected/noen-uul>), Tuv Province (aimag). They are counted among the major archaeological discoveries of the 20th century. The "Xiongnu carpet," woven portrait and silver items found here reveal Xiongnu art and contacts along the ancient Silk Road.

![image](asset:tic-img-880d9fd93126076c)

## What are the Noen Uul Tombs? Key facts

- **Location:** Noen Uul, Tuv Province, at the head of the Khara River. The tombs lie at the heads of three valleys: Sujigt, Juramt and Khujirt.
- **Discovered:** 1912, by A. Bollad, a technician of the Mongolor company.
- **Tombs:** About 200.
- **Main excavation:** 1924–1925, the expedition of P.K. Kozlov.
- **Notable finds:** the "Xiongnu carpet," a Chinese cup from 1 CE, a woven portrait of a warrior, and more than 30 silver items.

## How were the Noen Uul Tombs discovered?

In 1912, A. Bollad, a Buryat technician of the gold-prospecting company Mongolor, noticed many hollows in the forest of Noen Uul. They lay at the heads of the Sujigt, Juramt and Khujirt valleys, at the source of the Khara River, 130 km from Orgoo, the capital of Bogd Khan's Mongolia. A prospecting team from the Russian Academy of Sciences planned to come, but World War I delayed the visit. In 1924–1925, the Mongol-Tibetan expedition led by P.K. Kozlov found about 200 tombs at Noen Uul. From Tomb 6, it excavated extremely rare finds.

## How did the Xiongnu bury their nobles?

Excavations showed that Xiongnu nobles were buried with their belongings in plank coffins. The coffins stood inside a double log chamber, 7–12 m deep, with an entrance passage on the south side. In 1925, a special team from the Academy of Sciences of the Soviet Union came to assess the importance of Kozlov's finds. It excavated 92 tombs in the [Tuul River](</destination/heritage/place/tuul-gol>) valley and collected material from the New Stone Age. The team established that the Noen Uul tombs were Xiongnu. In 1927, the Mongolist A.D. Simukov excavated tombs at Noen Uul. He found felt carpet fragments, hammered gold sheets with plant patterns, a gilded flat figure of a horse, scraps of silk, and a lacquered cup with a Chinese inscription. Reading the inscription confirmed that the cup was made in Sichuan Province, China, in 1 CE.

![image](asset:tic-img-d54fe794f4a6510a)

## What makes the "Xiongnu carpet" so special?

The carpet from Tomb 6 became famous as the "Xiongnu carpet." It is skillfully decorated with images of wild animals and predators.

- **Center.** A cloud pattern.
- **Border.** A Chinese silk edging with a narrow band of wave patterns and oval-rectangular patterns.
- **Imagery.** Inside the border are yaks, deer, and tigers with manes and tails, along with fantastic creatures with animal ears and heads, wings and tails, shown locked in combat.

No other carpet with such elaborate imagery has been found elsewhere. The carpet shows how people of that time saw the world. The images of deer and wolves at play and the spiral patterns are more than simple decoration. The carpet is still admired today for the great skill of its sewing and embroidery. It is kept in the National Museum of Mongolian History.

## What else was found at Noen Uul?

- **Warrior portrait.** The woven portrait, made with a needle-threading technique, shows a Xiongnu noble. He has a mustache and cheek whiskers, long flowing hair, a prominent nose, layered eyes and a sharp gaze, giving him a heroic look.
- **Silver items.** More than 30 silver items were found. They include argali, Siberian ibex and one-horned gazelles made of silver, a gilded dragon, and ornaments with male and female figures in ancient Greek and Roman style.

Researchers say the burial chamber was covered with two layers of clay and charcoal. The clay soil of Noen Uul also does not let water through. As a result, all the items were well preserved.

## How did the Noen Uul Tombs become known worldwide?

In 1926, a London magazine in England published an article titled "Find of the earliest historical remains of a Mongolian state." Items found in Xiongnu tombs show that ancient Greek and European goods reached the Xiongnu along the Silk Road. The first person to excavate and study Xiongnu tombs was Yu.D. Talko-Grynsevich, who excavated about 100 tombs. Xiongnu monuments are found in many places in Mongolia: Noen Uul, Takhiltyn Khotgor, [Gol Mod](</destination/heritage/place/gol-mod-ii-khunnu-bulsh>), the Eg River, [Morin Tolgoi](</destination/heritage/place/morin-tolgoin-bugan-khushuu>) in the Tuul River valley, and [Baga Gazryn Chuluu](</destination/landscapes/baga-gazryn-chuluu>).

They are in Noen Uul, Tuv Province, at the head of the Khara River, where the Sujigt, Juramt and Khujirt valleys begin.

### Who excavated the Noen Uul Tombs?

The expedition of P.K. Kozlov excavated and studied them in 1924–1925, and A.D. Simukov did so in 1927.

### Where is the "Xiongnu carpet" kept?

It is kept in the National Museum of Mongolian History.

- **Discovered by:** Mongolian and Soviet archaeologists.
- **Material:** yellowish flint with an admixture of jasper.
- **Notable finds:** Acheulean-style handaxes and pointed bifacial tools.

## What tools were found at Yarkh Mountain?

Many kinds of tools were found, made from yellowish flint with an admixture of jasper:

- flakes struck from tool blanks;
- hard blades with a triangular cross-section;
- cores with one or two striking platforms;
- tools of the denticulate and scraper types;
- most interesting of all, handaxes and pointed bifacial tools.

### Handaxes

The handaxes are fairly massive. Both flat sides were flaked and shaped, and the edge was worked by striking alternately from each side. As a result, they have a wavy edge like European handaxes.

### Pointed bifacial tools

The pointed tools, shaped on both sides, resemble the "Micoquian" cultural remains of the European Lower Paleolithic. They are somewhat thinner, flatter and smaller than the handaxes.

## Why is the Yarkh Mountain site important?

In its toolmaking technique and in every other respect, the Yarkh Mountain site matches the handaxes and pointed bifacial tools called Acheulean, which were widespread across Asia, Europe and Africa in the Paleolithic. This is directly linked to when people first settled Mongolia, including the presence of humans with a pre-Neanderthal appearance. It is also important for clarifying how culture developed in Mongolia in the early Paleolithic.

### How did the site test Movius's theory?

Before the 1970s, some Paleolithic researchers followed the "theory of local development in the Stone Age," put forward by the American scholar H. L. Movius. According to it, in the Lower Paleolithic there were two large cultural zones. In the west lived people with handaxes. In South and Southeast Asia lived people whose tools were made by breaking smooth pebbles across and sharpening one edge. The Yarkh Mountain discovery widened the range of handaxe makers considerably. It became beyond doubt that Central Asia, including Mongolia, belongs to the same zone as Africa, Western Europe, Inner Asia and South India, the home of the "classic" handaxe makers. In this way, the Yarkh Mountain site played an important role in updating the understanding of the origins of Lower Paleolithic culture in Asia and Europe at that time.

### Oklandikov's assessment

The archaeologist A. P. Okladnikov wrote that the Yarkh culture, with its clear Acheulean features, is the first and only such site in East Asia, apart from Europe, the Caucasus, India and Africa. In his view, the Yarkh handaxes, in Acheulean technique and form, cannot be compared with the occasional so-called "handaxes" found in northern China and Korea.

## Why are European and Mongolian handaxes so similar?

One feature of the Yarkh site is that the makers of handaxes and pointed bifacial tools had a set method, like Lower Paleolithic people in the west, of shaping stone into tools worked on both sides. Researchers say it is wrong to explain the similarity between Mongolian and European handaxes by saying these distant peoples were related or influenced each other. It is more reasonable to see two cultures that look alike but developed separately. People in Asia and Europe each came up with the same idea of shaping stone into handaxes on their own. In this way, the stone tool workshop at Yarkh Mountain told a truth of unwritten history and became a major scientific discovery.

### How old is the Yarkh Mountain site?

It dates to the Lower Paleolithic, about 300,000 years ago.

@@ FIELD body richText
The open-air settlement is one of the most common concepts in the study of Mongolia's Stone Age. It refers to a site where tools, weapons, and other objects left by ancient people remain exposed on the ground surface. More than 80 percent of the over 800 Stone Age camps, settlements, and workshops found in Mongolia are open-air settlements.

## What is an open-air settlement? Key facts

- **Meaning:** a Stone Age site whose cultural layer lies exposed on the ground surface.
- **Share:** more than 80 percent of Mongolia's over 800 Stone Age sites.
- **Examples:** Moilt Valley, the settlements along the Sagsai River, Orkhon I and VII, and Tsakhiurt Valley.
- **Stages of formation:** three.

## Which open-air settlements are there in Mongolia?

Examples of open-air settlements in Mongolia:

- [**Moilt Valley**](</destination/heritage/place/moiltyn-amny-khuuchin-chuluun-zevsgiin-ueiin-buuts>)**.** Studied in 1961–1969 and in 1983.
- [**Settlements 1–12 along the Sagsai River**](</destination/heritage/place/sagsai-golyn-chuluun-zevsgiin-dursgal>)**.** These Neolithic settlements are in Sagsai soum, Bayan-Ulgii Province. In 1983, the Mongolian-Soviet expedition for studying historical and cultural sites examined them.
- **Orkhon I and VII.** Excavated and studied in 1987–1990.
- **Tsakhiurt Valley settlement.** Located in Bulgan soum, Umnugovi Province, at the foot of [Arts Bogd Mountain](</destination/landscapes/arts-bogd-uul>). It was discovered in 1995. Its place in world Paleolithic research, its contents, and its structure make it especially important.

## How do you recognize an open-air settlement?

The idea of an open-air settlement comes from where and how the archaeological "cultural layer" is preserved. In Stone Age research, the "cultural layer" is very different from the "cultural layer" studied for later periods. Tools, weapons, and other objects made and used by people stay stable. They remain either where they were first left or where natural changes did not move them. The main feature of a Paleolithic open-air settlement is that its historical cultural layer lies exposed on the ground surface. For this reason, researchers need to study specifically how the cultural layer of an open-air settlement forms.

## How does the cultural layer form?

The cultural layer of an open-air settlement forms in three stages.

### Stage one: the period of human life

It begins when the first traces of human activity appear on the topsoil. These include various tools and weapons, the waste left from making them, and the bones of animals that ancient people ate. This stage ends when people leave the place.

### Stage two: the period of natural impact

This stage lasts relatively longer than the first. Rain and strong sunlight cause the objects at the site to lose their original shape. Organic material, especially animal bones, is clearly affected. In addition, wild predators come to the abandoned site and consume the leftover bones and anything edible. In the end, only the stone tools made by human hands remain. Even stone tools, however, wear down from sunlight, rain, temperature swings, and wind. Some open-air settlements are buried by shifting soil, and a new layer of topsoil begins to form on top.

### Stage three: the period of lasting decay

This stage lasts much longer than the previous two, and the site remains in a state of gradual destruction. It ends when researchers discover the site.

## What is found at an open-air settlement?

The cultural layer of a Stone Age open-air settlement contains the following:

- Stone Age artifacts;
- fragments of early, poorly fired clay pottery;
- stones brought from elsewhere that kept their shape, such as stones set in a ring beside a fire;
- animal bones;
- charcoal and soot.

After people leave, wild predators consume the bones and anything edible. Sun and rain also destroy organic material. As a result, mostly stone tools remain.

## Where is Tsakhiurtyn Valley and what is it like?

The site lies in a low area between [Arts Bogd](</destination/landscapes/arts-bogd-uul>) and the Gurvansaikhan Range. The landscape is varied, with sand dunes, mounds, hillocks, ravines, gullies, and deep gorges side by side. In the central part, a raised plateau sits on limestone deposits. Its top and slopes are completely covered with flint-type stone. This flint-rich rock was clearly the main raw material that ancient people used to make tools and weapons. The rock on the topsoil shows traces of having first been split by human hands, which proves this. The site spans a very long period of time. Sources note that it contains remains dating back to the early Paleolithic. Researchers regard it as an open-air site with Paleolithic remains.

## How are the stone tools classified?

The stone tool remains are divided into three groups according to how much they have been worn by natural weathering.

### Heavily weathered remains

After being baked under the harsh Gobi sun for hundreds of years, the first flake scars are barely visible. The surfaces have become simple, wavy, and uneven. It is hard to tell what kind of tool each piece was from its striking platform and surface.

- The first flaking was done using a parallel or double-parallel method.
- Large and medium cores with a single striking platform, flaked from one side, are most common.
- The striking platform was prepared by removing a large flake from the stone.
- The blades and flakes struck from the cores are large and medium in size.

### Moderately weathered remains

The pits caused by weathering are relatively few, and the original edges of the flake scars are fairly clear.

- The first flaking was done using the transverse-longitudinal, parallel, and Levallois methods.
- Cores with transverse-longitudinal flaking seem to have been used to make large tools.
- The striking platform was made evenly.

### Lightly weathered remains

These are tools whose surfaces have only just begun to show traces of weathering.

- The first flaking was done using a lateral flaking method.
- The remains still have a single striking platform and are flaked from one side, while the cores have become multi-faceted (prismatic) and Gobi-type in shape.
- The condition of the striking platform shows that flaking began to be done in a circular pattern.

These differences in weathering show that the site was used over a long period of time. They also show that the methods and technology of tool making changed gradually.

## Why is Tsakhiurtyn Valley important?

Research in Tsakhiurtyn Valley can reveal how Stone Age people spread and settled across the vast area from the Arts Bogd Range south to the Gurvansaikhan Range. If the site is seen as a Stone Age "workshop," researchers can study the whole development of stone tool technology. This covers the period from the late Paleolithic to the early Neolithic.

A team from the joint Mongolian-Japanese "Inscriptions" project found more than 60 pages of birch bark writing from the 15th–17th centuries, along with three pages in Tibetan, buried under the rubble of Khar Bukhyn Balgas. According to the research team, these birch bark books:

The birch bark books are first being stabilized, preserved, and restored. The work is done in Mongolia, but if special equipment is needed, they are taken to Japan for restoration. Wangiin Tsagaan Mountain is an ancient sacred mountain in the area of [Khalkhgol soum](/province/dornod/khalkhgol) in [Dornod Province (aimag)](/province/dornod), just beyond the national border. It rises 1,099 m above sea level. It stands alone in the open steppe, so you can see it from 40–150 km away.

## What are the key facts about Wangiin Tsagaan Mountain?

- **Location:** Khalkhgol soum, Dornod Province, beyond the national border.
- **Height:** 1,099 m above sea level.
- **Visible from:** 40–150 km away.
- **Worship ceremony:** Stopped in 1937 and revived in 1990. It is now held every three years.
- **Nearby places:** Zurkhiin Tsagaan Khuuluu, Tsagaan Ovoo, Sangiin Dalai Lake.

## What is the history of Wangiin Tsagaan Mountain?

Around the mountain you will find places such as Zurkhiin Tsagaan Khuuluu, Tsagaan Ovoo and Sangiin Dalai Lake. The wise Khalkha Wang Togtokhtur once lived here with his people. The worship ceremony for the mountain was banned in 1937 and revived in 1990. Today it is held every three years.

## Why is it called Wangiin Tsagaan Mountain?

Locals tell an interesting legend about this mountain.

### The greed of the Manchu official

Legend says that in a hard time, when Mongolia was under Qing rule, a Manchu official tried hard to look diligent and clever to his emperor. This very proud official became greedy and wanted to make Mongolia's Tsagaan Mountain his own. The wise Khalkha To Wang was angry about this. He told him: "This mountain is exactly the size of one ox hide. You may take whatever extends beyond the hide. But if the two of us ever live as neighbors, I will not let your dog lick from my dog's bowl." The Manchu official did not understand the hidden meaning of these words and was delighted. He mocked To Wang in his thoughts: however big an ox hide might be, how much could it cover of a whole mountain? How did such a fool ever become a wang? He was sure the mountain would soon be his and that the emperor would reward his efforts. He said: "Agreed. Your people say a word spoken cannot be taken back, but a lost bet can be won back. The part of the mountain the size of one ox hide is yours. Everything beyond it is mine." So they agreed to meet three days later to divide Tsagaan Mountain.

### The cleverness of To Wang

The wise To Wang took a large bull's hide and had it cut around and around with a sharp knife into a thread-thin strip. This made a very long rope. To keep it from drying and shrinking, he stored the rope in water. On the agreed day, To Wang told the Manchu official, "Watch carefully." He unrolled the long rope and circled all of Tsagaan Mountain. About two arm spans of rope were left over. Then To Wang said sternly: "A thief has taken a large part of our mountain. They say my father hid gold in this mountain, meant as a gift for the emperor. That very part is now missing. You stole that gold and greedily wanted more. This is why you claimed the mountain as yours. I will report you to the emperor." Instead of a reward, the Manchu official now faced punishment. He panicked and said: "No, my noble official of heavenly descent, what could I lack that I would steal? Some other wicked person must have taken that gold. We must catch the thief." He handed To Wang two silver ingots and hurried back, the story goes.

### How did the name come about?

Wise To Wang kept the mountain of his homeland from a foreign official through his courage and wit. So people began to call it "Wise To Wang's Tsagaan Mountain." Over the years, "Wise To" dropped away, and the name stayed as "Wangiin Tsagaan Mountain."

Gunsavaa's Stone is in [Dornod Province (aimag)](/province/dornod), in [Sergelen soum](</province/dornod/sergelen>), north of the Gal River. In the 18th century, Gunsavaa of Lodongomb fought against the Manchu (Qing). This area is rich in legends and monuments linked to him. In Dornod, people still tell legends about places such as Gunsavaa's Stone, Ulaan Tolgoi, Gyalalzakhyn Pass and the Pine of Sandaa.

## What is there to know about Gunsavaa's Stone at a glance?

- **Location:** Dornod Province, Sergelen soum, north of the Gal River.
- **Monuments:** A rise with many graves called Shiveet, the foundations of four stone buildings measuring 10×8 m, and a square grave.
- **Related person:** Gunsavaa (Saikhansavaa) of Lodongomb.
- **History:** The 1755–1758 national struggle for freedom.
- **Related places:** Onti Khyar, Gyalalzakhyn Pass, Ulaan Tolgoi, the Pine of Sandaa.

## What is there to see around Gunsavaa's Stone?

North of the Gal River is a rise with many graves, called Shiveet. On its jagged northern spur are the foundations of four stone buildings, each 10 m long and 8 m wide. The square grave behind them is said to be where Gunsavaa's wife was executed.

### Onti Khyar

Legend says that Gunsavaa and Khonkhorbat held an archery contest between Shiveet on the Gal River and the Ikh Gazryn Stone. Their arrow pierced the summit of a height southwest of Bayan Mountain and made a hole. A hill with a hole near its top does exist there, and it is called Onti Khyar.

### Gyalalzakhyn Pass

Gyalalzakhyn Pass is in Bayan-Uul soum, Dornod Province. Legend says that Gunsavaa fought a fierce battle with Manchu troops, lost, and fled over this pass. As he crossed, his armor, bow and arrows, and quiver gleamed and then disappeared from view. This is how the pass came to be called "Gyalalzakhyn" (the gleaming one).

## Who was Gunsavaa of Lodongomb?

Gunsavaa was one of the patriots who lived in eastern Mongolia in the 18th century. He fought for independence and died in the struggle against the Manchu (Qing). His real name was Saikhansavaa. He was the son of Lodongomb, of the Khuan clan, from the northwest part of the banner (khoshuu) of Bei Achit in the Khalkha Setsen Khan province. Lodongomb's family lived around the Gal River, and his son grew into a tall, strong young man with the marks of a good warrior. Life was hard and poor, so at 18 Saikhansavaa went to serve as a hired soldier. In a war then taking place in western Mongolia, he reached the bank of the Orkhon River with the Khalkha forces. Saikhansavaa and Bat-Ochir scouted ahead of the main unit and captured one of the enemy's nobles. This brought the Khalkha army considerable success. In recognition, Saikhansavaa was granted the title "gun." This is how he became known as "Gunsavaa."

## How did Gunsavaa fight the Manchu?

After returning home, Gunsavaa resisted the Manchu firmly. He cut off their tribute and labor duties and took legal action against the nobles who served the Manchu. At that time, the armed struggle of Amarsanaa and [Chingunjav](</about/history/people/khotogoidyn-shadar-van-chingunjav>) for national freedom and independence, which swept the Mongolian people in 1755–1758, began. Gunsavaa took an active part in it. Mendiin Zenee, who studied Gunsavaa's life, wrote that the 1755–1758 uprising was a people's revolt across all Mongol ethnic groups. Amarsanaa led it in western Mongolia, Chingunjav in Khalkha Mongolia, Gunsavaa in eastern Mongolia, and Sevdenbaljir in Inner Mongolia. The struggle is often recorded as if it took place only in western Mongolia. The history of the armed struggle Gunsavaa led in eastern Mongolia stayed obscure until today. Gunsavaa is a hero who stands alongside Chingunjav of the [Khotogoid](</about/culture/ethnic-groups/khotogoid>), and he is remembered in the history of the Mongolian state and of the people of Dornod.

## How did Gunsavaa's life end?

By order of the Manchu emperor, Gunsavaa was executed. His head was to be shown to the emperor, so it was loaded onto a camel, balanced with a sack of tea. When the head was shown to the emperor, it is said that there was a span between Gunsavaa's two eyes. It is said that the emperor deeply regretted it and said, "What a pity. If he had been my subject, he would have been a most useful man."

He was Gunsavaa of Lodongomb, who fought against the Manchu (Qing) in the 18th century and led eastern Mongolia in the 1755–1758 uprising.
