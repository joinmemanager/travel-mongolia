The Khongiogiin River rock paintings are in [Erdeneburen soum](/province/khovd/erdeneburen) of [Khovd Province (aimag)](/province/khovd). They cover three sites in the Khongiogiin River basin: Alag Chuluu, Ikher Tolgoi and Khushuut. A joint Mongolian-Soviet historical and cultural expedition first discovered them in 1979.

## What are the Khongiogiin River rock paintings? Key facts

- **Location:** Khovd Province, Erdeneburen soum, Khongiogiin River basin.
- **Sites:** Alag Chuluu, Ikher Tolgoi, Khushuut.
- **Discovered:** 1979, by a Mongolian-Soviet expedition.
- **Technique:** pecked all over the surface by chipping the rock.
- **Period:** mostly the Bronze Age.

## What do the Alag Chuluu rock paintings show?

Alag Chuluu is a rocky outcrop with a winter shelter on the eastern flank of Alag Uzuur, in the second bag (the smallest administrative unit) of Erdeneburen soum. On more than fifty smooth rocks facing the sun, the artists depicted Siberian ibex, deer, wolves, foxes, roe deer, camels and cattle. They also show people on foot and on camels, and hunters with bows and arrows, alone and in groups.

### The main rocks

A flat rock in the center of the front face shows more than thirty Siberian ibex, and a deer or elk with its fawn. Almost all the animals face east. Most ibex horns are drawn with a single line, and in four cases the bends of the horns are rounded and emphasized. This way of highlighting the curve of ibex and argali horns is common across Mongolia and neighboring regions. At the top, a deer with upright horns is drawn fairly realistically, along with a fawn standing and looking at it.

### Hunting scenes

On one rock at the western edge, four hunters with short bows chase five ibex and one deer. Opposite them, an unarmed person scares the animals.

### Two oxen with a plow

On the vertical, flat rocks in the upper west, artists carved deer, ibex, wolves, a dog, a person on foot and cattle. Among them, one image stands out: two oxen with large horns are yoked to something like a plow and led by two people. It may show farming, which is thought to have begun in Mongolia in the Neolithic and developed further in the Bronze Age.

### Other images

- On a rock in the eastern part, two deer, one large and one small, stand facing east and are drawn fairly realistically. The large deer's antlers slant along its body with evenly balanced tines, and it is shown with its body drawn in.
- On the small rock farthest east, there are an ibex and a person riding a camel. The rider is one piece of evidence that people had begun to use camels for riding.

## What are the Ikher Tolgoi rock paintings like?

Ikher Tolgoi means "twin hills." These are two small hills at the start of the pass east of Alag Chuluu. On the southwest-facing wall of the southwestern hill, ibex, deer and predators are pecked into the rock in six places. The overall look, technique, subjects and composition match the Alag Chuluu paintings, and they date to the Bronze Age.

## What are the Khushuut rock paintings like?

Khushuut lies 20 km east of the two sites on the Khongiogiin River. It is a hill with four small rocky outcrops lined up from east to west. The place seems to be named for several monuments here, where statues more than three meters tall stand inside square stone enclosures. On almost every rock of the four hills, there are very many pecked images of ibex, and occasionally deer, wolves and other animals. One scene shows nine mounted hunters chasing ibex, wolves and other animals and aiming short bows at them. The mounted hunters, especially their movement, are drawn with great skill. These paintings date to the Bronze Age. For more, read the page on the [Khushuut rock paintings](</destination/heritage/place/khushuutiin-khadny-zurag>).

## What is Shargaljuut hot spring? Quick facts

- **Location:** Erdenetsogt soum, Bayankhongor Province, about 650 km from Ulaanbaatar.
- **Elevation:** 1,500 m above sea level.
- **Springs:** More than 100.
- **Water temperature:** 43–95 °C.
- **Composition:** Bicarbonate, carbonate and sulfate sodium.
- **Capacity:** 90 people per session. More than 3,500–4,000 people receive treatment each year.

## What makes Shargaljuut hot spring special?

The spring complex has more than 100 springs that stand out for their heat. They rise from among the granite boulders on the slope of a hill called Khatad, which pushes into the Shargaljuut River. They emerge through cracks in the granite, driven by the Earth's inner heat, the movement of groundwater, strong pressure and the contrast of hot and cold. It is a natural formation in its original state. The most remarkable feature is that each spring comes out from rocks shaped like a human organ. For example, the throat spring flows from a rock shaped like a throat, and the tooth spring from a rock shaped like a tooth.

## What is the water made of?

Studies show that most springs belong to one type. Some differ in heat and reaction to their surroundings, but bicarbonate, carbonate and sulfate sodium dominate, and the water temperature ranges from 43 to 95 °C. The spring southwest of the main source is 60 °C and has sulfurous mud, so you can combine spring and mud treatments. In composition, Shargaljuut is similar to springs such as Herculane in Romania, Gorna Banya and Kyustendil in Bulgaria, Kuldur in Russia and Talaya in Eastern Siberia.

![image](asset:tic-img-79aa2c03bbd2b79a)

## Which illnesses is Shargaljuut hot spring good for?

The water stimulates the nerve endings and blood vessels of the skin. This changes blood circulation, metabolism and the work of the endocrine system. For this reason, it is considered suitable for the following conditions:

- chronic joint inflammation
- some women's diseases
- some skin diseases
- respiratory diseases

Hot, lightly mineralized water raises body temperature and quickly eases inflammation. It is better suited to chronic diseases of the joints, muscles and bones.

### Who should not use it?

Using the spring is not advised in the following cases:

- advanced, recurring or complicated forms of cerebral artery hardening;
- shock, or the period after a severe heart attack;
- stage IIB or higher of high arterial blood pressure;
- permanent severe heart disease.

Talk to a doctor before using the spring.

## Who comes to the sanatorium?

The Shargaljuut sanatorium is open year-round. On average, more than 3,500–4,000 people receive treatment each year. Of these, 40.5 percent, or up to 1,850 people, come from Ulaanbaatar, and 470–600 come from Bayankhongor Province. Accommodation prices, health insurance, extra fees for patients without a referral, and phone numbers may change, so check with the sanatorium in advance.

## Where does the name "Shargaljuut" come from?

During Qing rule, couriers returning from Khovd and Uliastai had to cross a river called "Ikh Gol" (Great River). Once, a relay courier carrying urgent news arrived and found the water of the Ikh Gol red, swirling and foaming. After that, the Manchus began to call the river "Shar Galzuu Gol" (Yellow Mad River). Couriers delayed by even small things began to make excuses, saying "the Shar Galzuut River flooded," and so escaped punishment for delaying official duty. It is said that locals softened the name to "Shargaljuut" so that their river would not be called by a nasty name. One source says the Shargaljuut River is in Ulziit soum, Bayankhongor Province.

- **Lions.** Around the statue, 14 lion sculptures are linked by 208 iron chains. They express the idea that "Mongolia is an unbreakable fortress."
- **Renewal.** On March 1, 2011, the statue was recast in bronze. Then Prime Minister S. Batbold opened it.

## What is written on the base of the statue?

On the front of the base, the following words are carved in relief in the traditional vertical Mongolian script: "If our nation unites its will and strength and acts together with one purpose, there is nowhere we cannot reach. According to oral tradition, the People's Volunteer Army entered Khuree on July 8, 1921. Residents welcomed them with a formal ceremony, offering tea and platters of food. When General D. When work began on the statue for the 25th anniversary of the People's Revolution, Marshal Kh. Choibalsan had soldiers search for the spot of the stake. Yanjmaa. When the foundation stone was laid, Yanjmaa was overcome by memories and began to cry. Marshal Choibalsan handed her that white handkerchief.

The Chikhen Cave Stone Age site is in Bayan-Undur soum, [Bayankhongor Province (aimag)](/province/bayankhongor), 30 km north of the Shinejinst soum center. Early people lived for a long time in [Chikhen Cave](</destination/landscapes/chikhen-agui>), a limestone cave, from the end of the Middle Paleolithic to the Neolithic.

## Where is Chikhen Cave and what are the key facts?

- **Location:** Bayankhongor Province, Bayan-Undur soum, 30 km north of the Shinejinst soum center.
- **Size:** 9.5 m long, 5 m wide, 2 m high.
- **Discovered:** 1987, by a Mongolian-Soviet expedition led by A. P. Derevianko and D. Dorj.
- **Excavation:** Since 1995, by a joint Mongolian-Russian-American expedition.
- **Cultural layers:** four.
- **Periods:** end of the Middle Paleolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic.

## What is the setting of Chikhen Cave?

The cave mouth faces southeast, so the sun shines in for most of the day. The floor slopes toward the entrance. The cave is 9.5 m long, 5 m wide and 2 m high. It is not very large, but it is an excellent place to live. Excavations revealed four cultural layers that preserve traces of human life. These include a large number of stone tools, hearth pits where fires burned for a long time, animal bones and plant remains.

## What tools were found in Chikhen Cave?

Several thousand stone tools, with primary and secondary working, were found in the layers. They belong to the microblade tool type, and blade tools are the main feature of this settlement.

- Except for a few scrapers made from large blades and blade-flake pieces, tools were made from small blades.
- The people who lived here mainly aimed to make tools with serrated edges.
- They widely used sandy flint, chert and agate.

## How old is Chikhen Cave?

The dates of the first three layers were set by radiocarbon analysis of charcoal from hearths:

- **Layer I:** 8055 ± 155 years.
- **Layer II:** 8940 ± 100 years.
- **Layer III:** 11,160 ± 160 years.

This is very important for research. Earlier, [open-air sites](</destination/landscapes/neelttei-suurin>) found in Mongolia with wedge-shaped cores and tools with serrated blades were assigned to the Neolithic, and dated to no more than 6,000–7,000 years ago. The Chikhen Cave excavation, however, made it possible to conclude that microlithic stone tool production in Mongolia began at the end of the Upper Paleolithic and the Mesolithic.

### The fourth layer

The stone tools of the fourth layer differ sharply in form, size and material. Researchers consider that they can be linked to the Upper Mousterian. They are very similar to finds from the [Orog Lake](</destination/landscapes/orog-nuur>) site and to the lower layer of the Orkhon-1 site, which was dated to 38,600 ± 800 years.

## Why did early people live in Chikhen Cave?

The favorable natural and ecological conditions of the area explain why people lived in Chikhen Cave so long ago:

- **Raw materials.** Chert, agate and flinty sandstone for making tools were close by.
- **Hunting.** People hunted animals that came to the spring at the foot of the mountain. 350 m from the spring, there is a narrow gorge 100 m long, with high, steep rock walls on both sides. It is said that this gorge drove animals from the mountain behind the cave toward the water, which made hunting easier.
- **A good cave.** It gets plenty of sun, is dry and sheltered, and is close to water.

So Chikhen Cave has been shown to be a major site. It preserves remains from three Stone Age periods, from the end of the Middle Paleolithic through the Mesolithic and Neolithic, and early people lived here for a very long time.

Sanbeisiin Khuree was the main monastery of the Chin Achit Wang banner (khoshuu) in Setsen Khan Province (aimag). It stood in the area of present-day [Choibalsan](</province/dornod/choibalsan>), the center of [Dornod Province](/province/dornod), at a place called Tumen-Ulziit ovoo (sacred stone cairn). It was a major center of trade, economy and religion in Eastern Mongolia. It stood at a crossroads of the routes to Chuulalt Gate, China and Ikh Khuree.

## What is Sanbeisiin Khuree? Key facts

- **Location:** Dornod Province, Choibalsan, Tumen-Ulziit ovoo.
- **Founded:** 1821, during the time of Zasag Beis Minjuurdorj.
- **Structure:** 7 large datsans (monastic colleges) and 11 temples in total.
- **Monks:** About 1,000 lamas.
- **Destroyed:** 1939.
- **Revived:** Some temples and prayer services since 1990.

## How was Sanbeisiin Khuree founded?

In 1821, during the time of Zasag Beis Minjuurdorj, the monks began to hold a Tsogchin assembly every day on the slope of Tumen-Ulziit ovoo. They also held a Sojin assembly every two months and a Khailan assembly every year. This was the start of the monastery. In 1830 the first datsan, Tsanid, was built. In 1832 the Jud datsan was founded. From 1835, it became a custom to light a thousand lamps and hold a great assembly each year. In 1837 the monks began to recite the Ganjuur and Danjuur, written in ink of ground cinnabar, as well as the Yeruul and Khangal texts. After that, the prayer halls and temples gathered below Tumen-Ulziit ovoo. That same year, the Manchu Qing state awarded the "Shashin Ukhaant" temple.

## How did the monastery grow?

- **1842.** Manba datsan.
- **1844.** The Jambaalin, Choinkhorlin, Namjilin and Jamyanlin temples were built in the four directions around the "Shashin Ukhaant" temple. That same year the monks held a Tsogchin assembly below Tumen-Ulziit ovoo and founded the Agva datsan.
- **1862.** The Tsanid datsan for prayer assemblies.
- **1865.** The "Sain Bilgiig Badruulagch" temple.
- **1869.** The Duinkhor datsan, during the time of Zasag Beis Tserendorj.
- **1878.** The Dambadarjaalin and Sundevdarjaalin temples, on either side of the "Sain Bilgiig Badruulagch" temple, during the time of Zasag Beis Lkhavaanregzen.
- **1893.** The Toisom Darvaalin temple.

From 1903, the monastery held major Buddhist ceremonies. These included the Duinkhor tsam dance, the [Myal Bogd](/about/culture/religion/myal-bogd) tsam dance, and, from 1915, the Chogor tsam dance and the Yamandag sachog rite.

## What was Sanbeisiin Khuree like?

Sanbeisiin Khuree had 7 large datsans, 11 temples in total, a tradition of more than 10 large assemblies, and about 1,000 lamas. For example, it had:

- Choir, which studied Buddhist philosophy;
- Mamba, for medicine;
- Duinkhor, for astrology;
- Jud, which studied the secret tantric rites of Buddhism.

Oral accounts say the monastery had four temples representing four regions: Southwest, Northwest, Southeast and Northeast. About 1,000 lamas took part in the banner assembly. About 700–800 joined major assemblies, and 400–500 joined small special assemblies to recite the texts.

## How wealthy was Sanbeisiin Khuree?

Sanbeisiin Khuree was among the wealthy monasteries. Its funds came from offerings by the people and from labor and tax duties. In 1898 alone, the people of the banner supplied goods worth 89,770 units of brick tea to the monastery. This made life harder for ordinary people and caused resistance, as records show.

## What is left of Sanbeisiin Khuree?

In 1939 the monastery was completely destroyed. No foundations remain. However, research shows the site of one temple, measuring 25×22 m, next to a family's courtyard. Since 1990, local people have revived some temples and prayer services out of faith. An old image of Sanbeisiin Khuree is kept at the [Dornod Province Museum](</things-to-do/museums/dornod-aimgiin-muzei>) in Choibalsan. This information is based on "A Brief History of Dornod Province" (A. Damdinsuren, 2001), "The Crystal Mirror of Dornod" (2006), and a 1996 interview with Gonsyn Gendendorj, an elder born in 1921.

## Where is Khoid Tsenkher Cave? Key facts

- **Location:** Khovd Province, 25 km west of the center of Mankhan soum.

- **Rock:** whitish-gray limestone marble (carbonate rock).

## What is the cave's structure like?

The cave is made of carbonate rock, or whitish-gray limestone marble. The ceiling curves outward from the inside, so it has a slightly domed top. The rock walls are blackened by fire, which suggests that people lived here for a long time.

## What paintings are in the cave?

The artists first struck the outlines with a flint chisel, then colored them with red ocher and earth pigments. Many animals are shown on the walls and ceiling:

- ostriches, other large flightless birds and cranes;
- camels and mammoths;
- large-horned bison standing with their heads lowered;
- spotted-horned gazelles;
- snakes and trees that look like spruce.

Of the extinct animals, the mammoth draws the most attention. - **Researchers' conclusions.** Researchers have concluded that "the beginnings of Asian art lay in Central Asia, in the land of Mongolia." It is also called "the hearth of art from the era of Central Asian tribal unity."

## Moilt Am: key facts

- **Location:** [Uvurkhangai Province (aimag)](/province/uvurkhangai), northwest of the Kharkhorin soum center, on the north bank of the Orkhon River.
- **Discovered:** 1949, by the joint Mongolian-Soviet historical and ethnographic expedition led by A.P. Okladnikov.
- **Excavations:** 1960, 1961, 1964 and 1965.
- **Cultural layers:** four, with a total thickness of 1.5–2 m.
- **Period:** from the early Upper Paleolithic to the beginning of the Mesolithic and Neolithic.

## Why is Moilt Am important?

Thousands of stone tools and weapons found here show that people lived at this place over a long span of history. It was the first site in Mongolia with at least four cultural layers, so it has special scientific value. After its discovery in 1949, research teams led by A.P. Okladnikov, A.P. Derevianko, D. Dorj and D. Tseveendorj excavated and studied it. The excavations revealed four cultural layers, covering in order the early Upper Paleolithic through the beginning of the Mesolithic and Neolithic.

## What was found in the excavations?

Hundreds of stone items were found that clearly show the daily work of people at that time:

- cores;
- chopping tools of the chopper type;
- scrapers, side scrapers, awls and knives;
- pointed tools;
- many flakes and blades, both retouched and unretouched.

## How do the layers differ?

### Fourth layer

The lowest layer consists of yellowish, limy, clayey soil. Large flakes, blades, and Levallois-type cores with one or two striking surfaces were found here. Pointed tools and disc-shaped cores were few. Choppers worked on one or both sides, and chopping tools, were relatively common. Finds from this layer are assigned to the Levallois stage of the Paleolithic.

### Third and second layers

These layers fully show the advanced, gradual development of the Upper Paleolithic in Mongolia. Most tools were made from pebbles. Even so, it is clear that makers shaped Levallois-type two-surface cores and leveled the striking surface lengthwise. This produced elongated Upper Paleolithic cores with one or two striking surfaces.

- It became easier to strike blades from such cores. The blades had regular edges, were compact and were more convenient to use.
- The number of tools made from flakes and blades rose sharply, and small tool types such as scrapers became more varied.
- The outer shape of the cores changed and became truly classic and pointed.

### First layer

The uppermost layer has light brown humus soil and starts at the ground surface. Geologically it belongs to the Holocene, and by archaeological periodization to the Mesolithic. A huge number of flakes and blades were found here. Pebble tools also appear, but there are fewer than in the lower layers. Light items such as scrapers, small scrapers and awls made from small flakes show finer, more skillful workmanship.

## What can I see there today?

Beyond the excavation area, you can find many stones split by human hands, natural raw material for making stone tools, and cores lying on the ground.

## How were the Gurvan Mandal images made?

The flat, smooth rock surfaces on the mountaintops hold many pictographs and tamga marks made by people over many periods. The images were made in two main ways:

- pecking out the whole figure or its outline;
- scratching the image with a sharp point.

## What do the pecked images show?

- **Wild animals.** Deer, roe deer and Siberian ibex.
- **Livestock.** Horses, camels and dogs.
- **Human activity.** People armed with bows and arrows hunting, and people taming and training animals.

- **Flying deer.** This deer has a long, narrow, bird-like snout and antlers that curve back along its body, in a flying pose. It is stylized in the same way as on deer stones of the Bronze and Iron Age.

- Some images show Buddhist temples and monasteries, with their proportions and details.

- Some scenes show the daily life and religious rites of later people.

For this reason, these images can be linked to the 13th to 19th centuries.

Two of the three mountains have inscriptions in ancient Turkic runic script. Natsagdorj pioneered and developed new literary genres. These include lyrical essays, novellas, short stories, prose pieces, and plays. Every work is full of love for his homeland and his people. He was also the first to introduce Russian and world classics to Mongolian readers. He translated A. S. Pushkin's "The Queen of Spades" and "The Shot," and Edgar Allan Poe's "The Gold-Bug," from Russian and German. His works have been translated into many foreign languages. ## What is Khangarid? Quick facts

## What does the name Khangarid mean?

In Sanskrit, Khangarid, or samgarid, is explained as "legendary bird." The scholar Ya. Tsevel included "garid" in his dictionary and explained it as "a legendary bird and the king of winged creatures."

## How is Khangarid depicted?

It is said that this legend came to Mongolia through Tibetan Buddhism. Khangarid is described this way:

- It has human arms.
- In its right hand it holds a vajra (ritual scepter) marked with a Lantsa letter, and in its left hand a lotus flower.
- With the claws of its two lower legs, it grips and bites a snake.
- It has a swollen, large belly and two breasts on its chest.
- It has pig's ears and wears a rosary around its neck.

The snake symbolizes the suppression of harm caused by the earth and water spirits (lus).

## How has Bogd Khan Mountain been worshiped?

In 1778, in the 43rd year of the Tengeriin Tetgegch reign, Yundendorj, the minister and prince of Ikh Khuree, submitted a petition to the Manchu emperor. - **1779.** On the 19th day of the first summer month of the Yellow Pig year, the Manchu emperor approved the petition. He issued a decree that the four Khalkha provinces and their disciples together should worship Bogd Khan Mountain in summer and autumn, twice a year. With this, Bogd Khan Mountain was placed under protection for the third time.
- **Later.** Depending on the times and social conditions, worship became once a year.
- **After 1921.** The worship ceremonies stopped.

## How do the sutras describe the lord of Bogd Khan Mountain?

Agvaankhaidav, the Khamba Nomun Khan of Ikh Khuree, made a request to the Panchen Lama of Tibet. As a result, the book "Dashchiravyn San," or "Treasury of the Four Mountains' Blessings," was first presented. This text describes the sky lord of Bogd Khan Mountain as being of royal lineage. - His head is adorned with a white conch trumpet that turns clockwise, and white silk hangs from it.
- He rides a cloud-white horse with a golden saddle and silver bridle. He has a fierce face, sharp eyes and the look of a great khan.
- Ministers and princes who carry out his orders surround him. Khangarid birds gather above him, and beasts such as deer, musk deer, bears and wolves surround him below.

In Buddhist legend, the Khangarid bird crowning the sky lord's head is one of the powerful beings of the world. It has long remained a symbol of Bogd Khan Mountain.

The Khalkh River flows through eastern Mongolia and the northeast of Inner Mongolia. ## What are the key facts about the Khalkh River?

- **Source:** Greater Khingan Range.
- **Length:** 233 km.
- **Outflow:** Buir Lake.
- **Section in Mongolia:** Dornod Province, Khalkhgol soum.
- **History:** It is mentioned eight times in _The Secret History of the Mongols_.

## Where does the Khalkh River flow?

The end of the river splits into two branches. One branch empties into the northwest part of Buir Lake. The other joins the Orchun River, which rises just north of Buir Lake, and then flows into Hulun Lake in China.

## Where does the name Khalkh River come from?

There are two explanations for the name.

- **A shielding bank.** The river's west bank is higher than its east bank. Seen from the east, it looks like a wall built to shield the land. It is said that this is why people called it the Khalkh River.
- **A meaning of "wide."** Mongols call the wide wooden frame on each side of a doorway "khalkh." In western Mongolia, people still call the cheek, the widest part of the face, "khalkh." Across Mongolia, wide clothing and items are commonly described as khalkhgar or khalkhavch. In a Khingan praise poem, a wide river is said to flow from each of the four sides. The wide river that rises northwest of the Khingan Pass is the Khalkh River.

The name was already established by the late 12th century, as _The Secret History of the Mongols_ mentions it eight times. For this reason, the name is believed to have no connection to the Khalkha ethnic group. It is thought to be almost 500 years older than the Khalkha people.

## What happened to Chinggis Khaan at the Khalkh River?

Sections 175, 176, 191, 192 and 208 of _The Secret History of the Mongols_ tell of the Khalkh River.

- **Counting his troops.** Chinggis Khaan (Genghis Khan) went from Kharkhalzan Eleet to Dalan Nemurges. On the way from there to the Khalkh River, he counted his troops and found 2,600 men. Wang Khan had seized his other subjects, who had been grazing their herds in the homeland of the Three Rivers (otor, seasonal long-distance grazing).
- **Hunting.** Chinggis Khaan took 1,300 men and moved along the west side of the Khalkh River. They hunted for food.
- **The death of Khuildar Setsen.** [Khuildar Setsen](</about/history/people/khuildar-setsen>) had not yet healed from his wound. He ignored the advice of [Chinggis Khaan](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>) and chased gazelles and roe deer. His wound reopened and he died. He was buried at a place called Kheltgii Rock in Or Nuga on the Khalkh River.
- **Recruiting the Khongirad.** Chinggis Khaan moved downstream along the Khalkh River. He recruited the Khongirad people, who lived where the river meets Buir Lake, under Zurchidei Uruud, and took them along with his army.
- **Preparing against the Naiman.** He heard that the Naiman were coming to attack. Chinggis Khaan camped again at Kheltgii Rock in Or Nuga, counted his troops, and organized them.

In 1203, Chinggis Khaan camped at the Khalkh River. He escaped Wang Khan's pursuit, repelled winter and spring attacks, and prepared his forces. On the full moon, the 16th day of the first summer month of the Year of the Rat in 1204, he held a worship ceremony for his standard. He then rode from the lower Khalkh River toward the Kherlen River to meet the Naiman in battle.

## What is the nature of the Khalkh River Valley like?

The Khalkh River never dries up, even in the driest years. Its wide valley is rich in grass pastures, saline soil, wildlife such as gazelles and roe deer, and waterbirds and game birds. For this reason, people have traveled to the area and settled there since early times.

## What was the Great Repression?

More than 36,000 people were persecuted during the Great Repression. Of these, 17,000 were lamas, and the rest were military personnel and ordinary citizens. The repression began in 1922 under Prime Minister [Dogsomyn Bodoo](</about/history/people/dogsomyn-bodoo>) and lasted until 1960. It peaked in 1937. The aim was to keep Mongolia away from Japan and dependent on Russia in its economy, military and politics.

- **Special Extraordinary Commission.** Established in 1937, it was headed by Marshal Kh. Choibalsan, Minister of War. D. Luvsansharav, a member of the Small State Khural, and G. Tserendorj, Minister of Justice, served as members.

- **Persecution of lamas.** From 1937 to 1939, more than 17,000 lamas were killed as "internal enemies," and 767 Buddhist temples and monasteries were destroyed.
- **Khamb Ovoo.** In 2003, more than 600 skulls were found here. They are believed to belong to lamas who were shot en masse.
- **The "anti-party group."** In 1962, S. Loohuuz, O. Tserendorj and B. Ochirkhuyag were persecuted under this label.

- **Door of the "Bat Gun" prison.** This is the door of the prison where victims were held.
- **D. Bodoo board.** It shows the persecution of Prime Minister Dogsomyn Bodoo.
- **Thematic halls.** These cover the persecution of lamas, the persecution of intellectuals, and the "intellectuals' delusion" of 1940–1960.

## Sangiin Dalai: quick facts

- **Location:** Dornod Province, 240 km southeast of Choibalsan and 25 km north of Vangiin Tsagaan Mountain.
- **Shape:** oval, 3.2 km long and 2 km wide.
- **Table salt reserves:** 220.7 thousand tons.
- **Mirabilite reserves:** 5,860 thousand tons.
- **Research:** More than 10 Mongolian and Soviet scientists, 1943–1976.

## How was Sangiin Dalai studied?

From 1943 to 1976, more than 10 Mongolian and Soviet chemistry and geology scientists studied the lake. They found that it is important for the national economy.

- **Salt deposits.** Under the briny water of the lake's flooded area, there is a layer of table salt 2–15 cm thick.
- **Ancient lake.** The lake was found to have formed in the Early Jurassic period and was once quite large. Its depth reached up to 16 m.
- **Mirabilite.** Over time, through later changes, ice-like mirabilite up to 15 m thick crystallized.
- **Mud.** The lake basin holds valuable mud, and thanks to it the salt reserves keep growing.

### How is the salt extracted?

According to scientists' research, pure natural table salt can be obtained using the reservoir method. The deposited salt can be processed in several stages to produce extra-fine salt. At 20 °C, table salt forms crystals. Below 15 °C, table salt and mirabilite crystallize together.

![image](asset:tic-img-59394bc0551d6434)

## What does the legend say about how the salt in Sangiin Dalai formed?

Legend says that To Wang of the Khalkha firmly agreed with a Manchu (Qing) prince of the Inner Mongolian banner (khoshuu) to give a thousand calves in exchange for a thousand cartloads of salt. But the people of the Inner Mongolian banner arrived later than the agreed date. To Wang said, "You are late, so there are no thousand calves. Our need for salt has also been met. Take your salt and go back." The Manchu prince pleaded, "Why take such a heavy load back? Let us dump it here." At first To Wang refused, saying, "I won't let our land and water be polluted." After much pleading, he said, "Then I will show you where to dump it," and had the salt poured into the water around Sangiin Dalai. It is said that this is how the people of the east came to have a salt deposit where pure salt grows.

## What makes Mongolian salt special?

Salt used in food comes in two types: lake salt and mountain salt.

- **Composition.** About 96 percent of Mongolian lake and mountain salt is sodium. The remaining 4 percent consists of elements the human body needs, such as magnesium, potassium and sulfur.
- **Inexhaustible resource.** Lake salt grows and forms within the water, so it is considered an inexhaustible resource. Single-celled algae and various microorganisms in the water play a key role in forming the salt.
- **Harvest season.** Salt harvested in spring (April–June) and autumn (September–November) is of good quality.
- **Uses.** Besides food, 30,000 tons of salt a year are used in traditional medicine, power plant water softening, livestock feed supplements and leather processing.

## What legends are told about Kharuul Ovoo?

### The legend of the guard boy

Long ago, Jargalan was the homeland of the Zakhchin people. Subjects who served as the khan's guards lived there. - **Sukhbaatar.** 200 prisoners worked at the match factory.
- **Other sites.** State farms, the Nalaikh coal mine and a brick factory.

## When did the prisoners return home?

- **1991.** The Japanese side requested that the remains be returned.
- **1994.** Japanese specialists came, moved the remains to Khujirbulan, cremated them and took them back to Japan.
- **2001.** The name markers stayed where the remains had been, and a memorial complex was built in Dambadarjaa.

## How did Delgerkhangai Mountain get its name?

In early times, the mountain was called Inel Khan. "Inel" means "boundless, immense, the very peak of something great." Avtai Sain Khan was the son of Lord Onokh Uizen, who was the third son of Prince Gersenz Jalair. When he received the title Ochirbat Tushet Khan from the Dalai Lama, he issued a decree giving the title "King of Mountains," or Delgerkhaan, to Inel Khan, the main sacred mountain of his banner (khoshuu). Since then, it has been an object of great state worship and has been called Delgerkhangai.

## What is there to see around Delgerkhangai Mountain?

- **Altan Tevsh.** A large belly-shaped area right at the summit.
- **Tevshiin Tomb.** In the Khushootiin Am, on a small branch of the mountain called "Tevsh" on the slope side, there is an especially large tomb. Some researchers think it may be the burial place of [Chinggis Khaan](</about/history/people/chingis-khaan>).
- **Khuree Road.** A road that could be an ancient track runs along the ridge of that pass. Today it is called "Khuree Road."
- **Cave with an opening.** East of the tomb, on top of a small ridge, there is a cave with an opening. It is said that it connects to the tomb below.
- **Ongon.** In the valley behind the mountain, many thousands of horses graze each year on otor (seasonal long-distance grazing).
- **Green Hill.** A place behind the mountain where the dead have been laid to rest since early times. Researchers think servants who took part in the burial of high-ranking people may have been put to death here.
- **Khuren Zaraa.** North of the Tevsh tomb there are several large tombs. They are believed to be the tombs of lesser khans. Under Mongol custom, descendants were buried at the head side of their forebears.

## How was Delgerkhangai Mountain worshiped?

The text "Sacrificial Worship of the Delgerkhangai Mountain, Master of the Great Land, Which Brings Down Prayers of Peace," written by Yonzon Khamba Damtsagdorj, describes the offerings made. They included a hero's armor, swords, a distinguished person's clothing and ornaments, seats and tables, and other items. Legend says the mountain's spirit (lus savdag) is a giant snake about 20 meters long, as thick as a sleeved arm.

## Why is Delgerkhangai Mountain so intriguing?

Many legends and research theories about state worship, ancient tombs and royal burials center on this mountain. Based on these, some researchers think Delgerkhangai Mountain may hold the sacred ongon (spirit shrine) of the Great Lord Bogd Khan.
